1984
George Orwell

CHAPTER 1
A Cold Day in April

The clocks of London were striking thirteen when Winston Smith came home to Victory Mansions in the middle of the day, turning his face down against a wind that carried grit and the smell of boiled cabbage through the streets. He was thirty-nine years old, small and thin, with fair hair and a face reddened by coarse soap and cold weather, and above his right ankle there was a varicose ulcer that made the seven flights of stairs a slow punishment. The lift seldom worked. The electricity was cut during the day, as it always was in the weeks before Hate Week, and so he climbed, resting at each landing beneath the enormous coloured poster that had been pasted to the wall: a face more than a metre wide, the face of a man of about forty-five, with a heavy black moustache and strong, rugged features, its eyes so arranged that they followed anyone who passed. Beneath it ran the caption: BIG BROTHER IS WATCHING YOU.

Inside his flat, a fruity voice was reading out a list of figures to do with the production of pig-iron. The voice came from an oblong metal plaque on the right-hand wall, like a dulled mirror, and Winston knew it well: the telescreen. It received and transmitted at the same time. Any sound he made above a whisper could be picked up by it; any movement within its field of vision could be watched. There was no way of knowing when one was being observed, and so the people of Oceania lived, by habit that had become instinct, on the assumption that every word was overheard and every movement scrutinised.

Winston turned his back to the screen, which was permitted, though even a back might be revealing, and stood looking out of the window at the world he belonged to. London, the chief city of Airstrip One, third most populous province of Oceania, lay grey and cold under a sky of the cruellest April. Far away, the Ministry of Truth rose white and enormous, three hundred metres of concrete pyramid, and on its face the three slogans of the Party were written in elegant lettering: WAR IS PEACE. FREEDOM IS SLAVERY. IGNORANCE IS STRENGTH. Beyond it stood the other ministries, similar in appearance: the Ministry of Peace, which concerned itself with war; the Ministry of Love, which maintained law and order; and the Ministry of Plenty, which was responsible for economic affairs. Their names in Newspeak were Minitrue, Minipax, Miniluv and Miniplenty, and Winston worked in the first of them.

His lunch hour was not truly his own, but he had come home with a purpose that had been growing in him for weeks, and the purpose frightened him. In the little alcove beside the window — a shallow recess left by some accident of the building's design — the telescreen could not quite see. It was the only square metre of his life that was unwatched. Winston sat down in the alcove with a book he had bought in a junk shop in the prole quarter: a thick, handsome volume with a smooth creamy paper that had not been manufactured for forty years or more, bound in red, its title page torn away. It was a diary.

To mark the paper was nothing in itself. Diaries were not forbidden, for there were no laws any longer, but if he were discovered, the punishment would be death, or at the least twenty-five years in a forced-labour camp. He had already bought a pen — a real pen, an archaic instrument with a nib, bought furtively because merely to feel its point on the paper seemed to him important. Now, with his hand unsteady, he wrote at the top of the page the date: April 4th, 1984. Then he stopped, seized by helplessness. What did one write? For whom was he writing? Not for any reader of the present, for there were none he could trust; not for the future, which might never be allowed to know him. He was writing, he understood at last, for no one — and that was precisely why he had to write.

The memory that had driven him home rose up again: the morning at the Ministry, the Two Minutes Hate, the faces around him, and the strange moment when his eyes had met the eyes of O'Brien. Words began to come, clumsy and frantic, his handwriting lurching across the page, first in capitals and then in a desperate scrawl, and what came out of him, over and over, was the cry that had been locked in his chest for years: DOWN WITH BIG BROTHER. DOWN WITH BIG BROTHER. DOWN WITH BIG BROTHER.

He wrote it half a dozen times before he could stop, and then he sat staring at the page in terror at what he had done. He had committed — would still have committed, even if he had never set pen to paper — the essential crime that contained all others in itself. Thoughtcrime, they called it. Thoughtcrime was not a thing that could be concealed for ever. You might dodge successfully for a while, even for years, but sooner or later they were bound to get you. The Thought Police plugged into your wire whenever they wanted to. They could watch you sleeping. They could even, he believed, see inside the skull.

There came a knock at the door. Winston froze, his heart hammering like a drum that would betray him to the whole street. It would be Mrs Parsons from the flat across the landing, wanting something borrowed or mended — it nearly always was. But for one endless moment he was certain it was them. He wiped his face on his sleeve, arranged his features into the expression of quiet optimism the Party approved of, and went to answer the door of his small, watched, ordinary life, with the diary shut inside its drawer and the crime already complete.

CHAPTER 2
The Two Minutes Hate

Every morning at eleven, the workers of the Records Department gathered before the great telescreen in the long room where Winston spent his days, and every morning the Hate began. It was not a thing one could opt out of. To arrive late, to stand at the back, to be seen not participating — all of it was noticed, and being noticed was the beginning of the end.

Winston took his seat between a heavy, sweating girl from the Fiction Department and a sandy-haired, rabbit-like woman named Mrs Parsons — no, not Mrs Parsons, that was his neighbour; this was a colleague whose name he never retained, a woman who shivered pleasurably through every session as though the screen's horrors were a warm bath. In front of him sat a girl with dark hair, a junior anti-sex sash at her waist — the scarlet sash of the Junior Anti-Sex League, wound tight about her hips as though to advertise her chastity. Winston disliked her, as he disliked nearly all women of the Party, and especially the young and pretty ones, who were always the most bigoted adherents of the Party, the swallowers of slogans, the amateur spies and nosers-out of unorthodoxy. Something in her brisk, athletic walk and her zealot's face made him certain that one day she would denounce him for some expression caught unawares.

On the screen appeared the face of Emmanuel Goldstein, the Enemy of the People. Goldstein had been the primal traitor, the earliest defiler of the Party's purity, an apostate who had escaped to some hiding place from which he directed, through a shadowy organisation called the Brotherhood, every act of sabotage and treachery in Oceania. As always, the sight of him set the room murmuring. The Hate was not a performance the audience watched; it was a weather they were caught in. Within thirty seconds, half the room was uttering exclamations of rage. The self-satisfied sheep-like face on the screen, bleating, and behind it the Eurasian enemy army marching — row after row of solid-looking men with expressionless Mongolian faces, dissolving and re-forming — drew cries and hissed curses from every throat. The little sandy-haired woman gave a squeak of mingled fear and disgust. Someone hurled a Newspeak dictionary at the screen. The Hate rose, as it always rose, to a crescendo of genuine fury, and Winston felt his own body swept along by it, his own hatred flowing as easily toward Goldstein as electric current through a wire — and then, at the peak, flowing just as easily away from him.

That was the horror of the Two Minutes Hate, and Winston understood it better than he wished to: not that one was obliged to act a part, but that it was impossible to avoid joining in. The rage that one felt was an abstract, undirected emotion that could be switched from one object to another like the flame of a blowlamp. One moment his hatred was not for Goldstein at all but for Big Brother, for the Party, for the Thought Police; at such moments his heart went out to the lonely, derided heretic on the screen, the sole guardian of truth and sanity in a world of lies. And the next moment he was at one with the people about him, and all that was said of Goldstein seemed to him to be true. It was even possible, he thought, that no one he knew hated the Party as he hated it — and yet the Hate could carry him like a tide, and for a moment he would be shouting with the rest.

At the end of the two minutes, the face of Goldstein dissolved into the face of Big Brother — black-haired, black-moustachio'd, full of power and mysterious calm — and the room sighed itself back to calm, and a voice from the screen cried out the slogans, and the workers returned to their cubicles as though returning from weather into a room.

But Winston remembered one thing from that morning above all others, and it was the thing that had driven him home to the diary. Across the room sat O'Brien, a member of the Inner Party, a large, burly man with a thick neck and a coarse, humorous, brutal face, who held some post so important and remote that Winston had only a dim idea of its nature. As the Hate reached its climax, Winston's eyes had happened to rest on O'Brien's face — and O'Brien, adjusting his spectacles on his nose in that characteristic gesture of his, had looked back. For a fraction of a second the two men's eyes met, and in that instant Winston knew — yes, he knew — that O'Brien was thinking the same thing as himself. An unmistakable message had passed. It was as though their two minds had opened and the thoughts were flowing from one into the other through their eyes. I am with you, O'Brien seemed to say. I know precisely what you are feeling. I know all about your contempt, your hatred, your disgust. But don't worry, I am on your side!

And then the flash of intelligence was gone, and O'Brien's face was as inscrutable as everybody else's. It might have been imagination. It might have been nothing at all. But Winston clung to it in the days that followed like a man in a shipwreck clinging to a spar, because in all the world he knew of no one else to whom he could say a single true word.

CHAPTER 3
The Memory Hole

Winston's work in the Records Department of the Ministry of Truth consisted, in the main, of rectification. Each day, bundles of directives arrived at his cubicle through the pneumatic tubes: back numbers of The Times that contained statements which had become incorrect, speeches of Big Brother whose predictions events had falsified, statistics from the Ministry of Plenty announcing abundance where hunger now had to be announced instead. Winston's task was to rewrite the past so that it agreed with the present, and to drop the originals down the slit in the wall beside his desk — the memory hole — from which they travelled to enormous furnaces somewhere in the bowels of the building. When the task was done, no trace remained that any alteration had ever been made. The new version was the past, and it had never been otherwise.

It was not, strictly, forgery. It was merely the substitution of one piece of nonsense for another. Most of the material he dealt with had no connection with anything in the real world, not even the kind of connection contained in a direct lie. Statistics were just as much a fantasy in their original version as in their rectified version. A great deal of the time, he was inventing as he went, guided only by what the Party required to have been true.

On the morning the diary entry was still fresh in his mind, a directive arrived that gave him an opportunity of a rarer and more pleasurable kind. The speech of Big Brother had contained a reference to an organisation which had never existed — a slip, or a trap — and Winston was required to eliminate the reference entirely and to supply, in its place, some wholly invented matter. He set about the work with relish. He created Comrade Ogilvy. Comrade Ogilvy, unimagined an hour earlier, came into existence fully formed: a Party hero who at the age of three had refused all toys except a drum, a sub-machine gun and a model helicopter; who at six had joined the Spies; who at seventeen had been a district organiser of the Junior Anti-Sex League; who at twenty-three had died in battle over the Indian Ocean, pursuing an enemy aircraft, carrying dispatches so important that he had weighted himself down with his machine gun and leapt from his helicopter rather than let them be captured, dying — as Winston wrote, and it was impossible to resist the phrase — with contempt for the enemy written on every feature. Winston added a citation of decoration by Big Brother himself. Within the hour, Comrade Ogilvy, who had never existed in the present, existed in the past, and would exist in it for ever, as unquestionably as Charlemagne or Julius Caesar. A photograph would be fabricated to accompany him. People would weep over him. And no one would ever be able to prove a word of it false, because the proof had gone down the memory holes of the world.

This was the thing Winston understood more clearly than any of his colleagues, and it terrified him in a way the telescreens never quite did: the Party did not merely demand obedience in the present. It claimed ownership of the past itself. If the Party could thrust its hand into the past and say of this or that event, it never happened — that surely was more terrifying than mere torture and death. And if all records told the same tale, then the lie passed into history and became truth. "Who controls the past," ran the Party slogan, "controls the future: who controls the present controls the past." The past, Winston thought, had not merely been altered; it had been destroyed. For how could you establish even the most obvious fact when there existed no record outside your own memory? He tried to recall whether London had always been as it was — the crumbling houses, the shortages, the rotten nineteenth-century streets — and found his memory full of holes, of brightly lit corridors with no past attached to them. Once, years before, he had held in his hands actual evidence: a photograph, clipped from The Times, showing three men — Jones, Aaronson and Rutherford, once eminent members of the Party, later executed as traitors after confessing to crimes committed on a date when the photograph proved one of them had been on the other side of the world. For a moment, the whole world had cracked open and shown him that the confessions were lies. He had destroyed the photograph, terrified, dropping it down the memory hole himself. But he had never forgotten it. The evidence existed in his mind alone, and a mind could be changed, broken, re-made. One day his memory would be as much a fabrication as Comrade Ogilvy, and there would be no one left alive who knew.

At lunch in the canteen — a low-ceilinged room smelling of sour beer and metal, where the food was a dull stew and the gin at the counter burned like kerosene — he sat with Syme, a philologist from the Research Department who was working on the Eleventh Edition of the Newspeak Dictionary. Syme was small, dark, precise, and terrifyingly intelligent, and he talked about Newspeak with the delight of a man sharpening a knife. "We're cutting the language down to the bone," he said. "Every year fewer and fewer words, and the range of consciousness always a little smaller. In the end we shall make thoughtcrime literally impossible, because there will be no words in which to express it." Winston listened, nodding, eating his stew, and thought with a kind of cold certainty: Syme will be vaporised. He was too intelligent. He saw too clearly and spoke too plainly. The Party did not like to have people like that. One day Syme would vanish, and his name would be removed from every list, and every word he had ever written would cease to have existed. Winston raised his spoon and ate, and kept his face arranged in the expression of a man who agreed with everything he heard.

CHAPTER 4
What the Proles Remember

Winston dreamed often of his mother. He had been ten or eleven years old when she disappeared, in one of the great purges of the nineteen-fifties, and he carried into middle age a wound of guilt that no rectification could rewrite: he remembered himself as a child, insatiably hungry in a time of scarcity, snatching food, screaming for more, and at last stealing the whole of his little sister's share of a ration of chocolate and running out of the door with it, down the stairs, away — and when he came back, his mother and sister were gone. He never saw them again. In the dream she sat far below him in a deep place, in the saloon of a sinking ship, with the little girl in her arms, looking up at him through water that grew darker and darker; and the gesture she made — her arm around the child, her face turned upward — contained a whole tragedy he could not have understood as a boy and could not stop understanding as a man. The proles, he thought, had remained human. The Party members had lost that. His mother had died loving him and holding his sister, in a world where private loyalty still meant something, and no power on earth could have made her betray the feeling, because she had nothing with which to reason about it. She had been governed by private loyalties, which the Party could not create and could not destroy in people like her — only in people like him.

It was this thought that drew him, in the evenings, down into the prole quarters, where the Party's writ ran thin. The proles were the vast majority of Oceania's population — eighty-five in every hundred — and the Party, which held them in contempt, let them live in a kind of animal freedom, without telescreens in most of their houses, policed by patrols but largely left to breed, work, quarrel, sing and die. "Proles and animals are free," said the Party textbooks, and because no one expected anything of them, they remembered things. If there was hope, Winston had written in his diary, it lay in the proles — not because they would ever revolt; they would not, until they became conscious, and they could not become conscious until after they had revolted. Still, they were the only human beings left who had lived before the Revolution and might compare the two worlds.

In a little pub with sawdust on the floor, he bought a pint of beer for an old man — a very old man, eighty at least, with white moustaches and a bent back — and tried to question him. "You've lived a long time," Winston said. "You must remember when you were a young man. Was life better then than it is now? Were we — was the world —" But the old man's memory, when he got it talking, was nothing but a rubble of meaningless details: a quarrel with a brother-in-law over a bicycle pump, the price of beer, the trousers worn by the toffs, a great storm, the names of public houses long demolished. He remembered everything except the one thing Winston needed to know: whether ordinary life had been better, whether a common person had been safer from arbitrary power, before the Party. The past had slid out of his head as surely as if the Ministry had rectified it. A million useless facts; not one usable answer. Winston paid for the beer and went out into the street understanding that the proles' memories were like his own — full of holes, and no use as evidence against a world that kept all the documents.

Near the pub stood a junk shop, dusty and dim, and in its window lay an object that caught him: a paperweight, a lump of glass with a piece of coral suspended inside it, beautiful with the useless beauty of another century. He went in and bought it from the elderly proprietor, a man named Charrington, with mild eyes and a manner left over from an older world, who showed him, at the back of the shop, a small room with no telescreen — "There never was one in here," Mr Charrington said, almost apologetically — furnished with a double bed and a print of a church on the wall. Winston looked at the room and at the paperweight in his hand, and felt something dangerous stir: the past, he thought, could be touched. It could be held. It could even, perhaps, be lived in. He did not yet know what he would do with that feeling. But he carried the paperweight home wrapped in paper, like a small round secret, and the coral inside it hung in the glass, pink and still, from a world where things were made to be beautiful and not to be believed.

CHAPTER 5
The Note

It happened in the corridor of the Ministry, on an ordinary morning, in full view of the telescreens, and it took less than ten seconds. Winston was walking toward the lifts when he saw the dark-haired girl from the Fiction Department coming toward him along the passage — Julia, he had heard her called, though he knew nothing of her except her work, her sash, and the suspicion he had nursed for months that she was watching him for the Thought Police. She was limping slightly, one hand bandaged, and as she came level with him she stumbled — or seemed to stumble — and fell, catching at his arm to save herself, and in the instant of contact he felt something small and hard pressed into his palm. He closed his hand over it without looking, helped her to her feet with every appearance of courtesy, murmured the expected words, and walked on with his heart slamming against his ribs.

It was a folded scrap of paper. For the rest of the morning it sat in his pocket like a live coal while he weighed the possibilities, and all of them were bad. It might be a message from the Thought Police. It might be a trap laid by the girl herself, an amateur spy's provocation. It might be a summons from some underground organisation — even, he hardly dared to shape the thought, from the Brotherhood. In the Records Department there was one safe place: his cubicle was full of paper, and a man handling slips all day could unfold one more without the screens remarking it. In the middle of a bundle of directives, with his face arranged in studious calm, he opened it. Written in a large, unformed hand were three words: I love you.

For several minutes he sat staring at his desk, unable to work, while the world rearranged itself around those three words. He had feared her; he had even, in idle hatred, imagined violence against her; and all the time she had been watching him for an entirely different reason. Nothing in his life had prepared him for this. Desire had been almost abolished in his world — his own marriage to Katharine, years ago, had been a dutiful, joyless arrangement blessed by the Party solely for the begetting of children, and it had ended in separation without bitterness, because bitterness would have required feeling. The Party encouraged the feeling that sex was a disgusting minor operation, like having an enema; it channelled all love upward, toward Big Brother, and all energy outward, into the Hate. What this girl was offering — recklessly, impossibly — was the thing itself, aimed at him.

Now he had to reach her, and reaching her was a problem of logistics as intricate as any military campaign. They could not be seen talking. They could not be seen not talking, if the not-talking looked arranged. For days he contrived small encounters that were not encounters: arriving at the canteen as she rose from a table, passing in corridors at hours when the traffic was thickest, learning with a spy's patience the pattern of her days. At last, in the canteen, he managed to sit at her table — drawn there, as it were, by chance, with other people between them and beside them — and there, over the clatter of trays and the stink of stew, with both of them staring at their food and speaking in voices pitched for the microphones, they planned a meeting the way generals plan an invasion. Victory Square, Sunday afternoon, in the crowd, where everyone would be and no one could be followed. Don't come near me yet, she murmured, not even looking at him. Stand where you can see me. He ate his bread, agreed, and walked back to his cubicle with the whole texture of his life changed.

The days between were the strangest he had ever lived. He did his work mechanically and well — better than well, for his hands seemed steadier and his mind clearer than they had been in years, as though the secret at the centre of him were a coal that warmed the whole body. He caught sight of Julia twice, at a distance, in a corridor and once across the canteen, and each time the discipline of not looking, of letting his eyes slide past her face as past a stranger's, cost him a physical effort, like holding a weight at arm's length. He rehearsed the journey in his head until he could walk it blindfold: the train, the lane, the square where the crowd would swallow them. He was not afraid, exactly. Fear was too small a word for a state in which every hour of his remaining life had been wagered on a scrap of paper and three words written in a girl's unformed hand.

On Sunday the square was a sea of people — a demonstration, columns of prisoners being marched through, the crowd surging and shouting — and in the middle of it Winston worked his way, inch by inch, until he stood at Julia's shoulder, and their hands found each other and gripped hard, hidden by the press of bodies, while the crowd roared at the prisoners on the screen. There, shouting with the mob, she gave him his instructions in fragments: the train to take, the station, the path, the place in the country where no telescreen could be and no patrol ever came; and the time. Two Sundays from now. Then the crowd shifted, their hands were torn apart, and he was alone in the roaring square with a map in his head and a rendezvous with the one thing in Oceania the Party had sworn did not exist.

CHAPTER 6
The Country of the Golden Bowl

The place Julia had chosen was a clearing at the edge of a wood, miles from any town, where the bluebells stood in a great pool of colour under the trees and a stream ran somewhere out of sight. Winston came by a route she had dictated — a train, a lane, a stile, a path through a copse — varying nothing, trusting everything, with his heart beating so hard on the walk from the station that he had to stop twice and pretend to tie his shoe. He was terrified, and beneath the terror ran a wild astonishment at himself: he was thirty-nine, with an ulcerated ankle and rotten teeth, a man who had never in his life done anything that was not observed; and here he was, committing adultery with a Party member in the open countryside, an offence for which people were sent to the Ministry of Love.

Julia arrived without a sound, coming through the trees with the practised quiet of someone who had done this before, and the sight of her dissolved his fear into something else. She was utterly unlike the sash-wearing zealot of the Ministry. She tore off the scarlet sash and flung it over a branch with a gesture of magnificent contempt; she had brought real chocolate, bought on the black market, and broke it for him; she laughed at the Party, at the slogans, at the whole vast fraud, with the ease of a person who had decided long ago to obey every rule outwardly and believe none of them. She explained herself as they sat among the bluebells. She was twenty-six. She lived in a hostel, worked on the novel-writing machines in the Fiction Department, marched in every demonstration, shouted at every Hate, organised for the Anti-Sex League — and all of it was camouflage, a performance of loyalty so perfect that no one looked twice at her. "I never meant it," she said. "Any of it. It's all rot, and I knew it was rot when I was fourteen. But you have to swim with the current, or they'd smell it on you." Her rebellion was not like his. She had no interest in the Brotherhood, in history, in whether the Party's claims were lies; she cared only for her own life and her own pleasures, seized now, in the present tense. Winston, listening, understood that she was wiser than he was in the way that mattered most for survival, and that her wisdom would never save either of them.

What passed between them in the clearing, in the long spring afternoon, was to Winston not merely an act of the body. He had been taught, and had half believed, that desire was the Party's enemy only when it was frustrated; that is why the Party frustrated it. Now he understood differently. This was a political act. Their embrace, when he held her close and felt her heart beating, was a blow struck against the Party — the first blow he had ever struck that cost him nothing in guilt and gained him something in joy. The animal in him, which the Party had spent his whole life trying to starve into service, had simply refused; and its refusal was a kind of victory. He told her about the diary. He told her about O'Brien, and the look that had passed between them. She listened with practical attention, neither thrilled nor afraid, and said that if O'Brien was what he hoped, they would need to be careful, and if he was not, they would need to be more careful. Then the light began to go, and they dressed, and walked back to the station by different paths, and the sash was wound again at her waist, and they were two loyal Party members who had never met.

After that they met when they could — in the crush of a crowd during Hate Week preparations, in a church belfry where she had once hidden, in the prole streets where two more shabby figures attracted no notice. Each meeting was stolen, brief and in constant danger, and each one enlarged in Winston a feeling that terrified him with its unfamiliarity: he was becoming happy. He began to sleep better. The ulcer on his ankle troubled him less. He caught himself, once, humming under his breath at his desk, and stopped in horror at the sound — and then, alone in the alcove that night, smiled at the memory of it. It could not last. Both of them knew it could not last, and neither of them said so. Julia, practical as ever, began to speak of a place — a room, in the prole quarter, over a shop, where an old man kept a bed and there was no telescreen. A place where they might meet not as fugitives in a wood but as people, behind a door. "I know the very shop," Winston said slowly. "I bought a paperweight there." And he felt the coral in the glass, somewhere at the bottom of his life, begin to glow like a coal.

CHAPTER 7
The Room over the Shop

Mr Charrington received the suggestion of rent without surprise, as though shabby Party members came to him every week asking for a room with no telescreen. The price was modest. The room was up a narrow stair at the back of the junk shop: a small, stuffy, beloved room, with a double bed under a patchwork quilt, a fireplace, a dresser, and on the wall a steel engraving in a rosewood frame — a church, with a square tower and a porch, which Mr Charrington identified as St Clement's Dane, and to which he attached, in a sing-song voice, the fragment of an old rhyme: "Oranges and lemons, say the bells of St Clement's." Winston repeated the words after him, charmed by them as he had been charmed by the paperweight. They were a piece of the abolished past, surviving in an old man's head like a fly in amber. The rhyme broke off unfinished — Mr Charrington could not remember the rest — and somehow that made it more precious, not less.

The room became their world. Julia came to it transformed, once, in a way that undid Winston completely: she had brought, smuggled piece by piece at mortal risk, real coffee, real sugar, real jam, a loaf of white bread such as no Party member ever saw — and she had brought a dress. She disappeared behind the screen in the corner and emerged not as a comrade in overalls but as a woman, in a velvet jacket and a silk scarf, with her face powdered and her lips coloured from little pots she had bought in the prole markets. "I wanted to be a woman, not a Party comrade, for once," she said. "For you." Winston, holding a cup of coffee that tasted like a memory of coffee, understood that this, too, was politics — the most dangerous politics there was. The Party had not merely forbidden their love; it had tried to abolish the world in which such a transformation made sense. Every painted lip was a heresy. Every hour in that room was stolen not only from surveillance but from an entire philosophy.

The first evening set the pattern of all the others. Julia arrived with her bag of treasures and an air of proprietorship that delighted him, inspecting the room like a housewife in a story from before the Revolution — shaking out the quilt, poking the fire, setting the paperweight where the light caught it, putting a bunch of stolen flowers in a jam jar on the dresser. She had a genius for making the place theirs, and within an hour the shabby little room had acquired the one quality no Party dwelling Winston had ever entered possessed: it felt inhabited by choice rather than assigned by a committee. He watched her move about it and thought that this was what the past must have been like everywhere — rooms that belonged to the people in them, doors that shut on the world, hours that were not accounted for. It was a kingdom of perhaps a dozen square metres, and it was the freest place in Oceania.

They talked there as they could talk nowhere else. Julia fell asleep easily and often, like an animal, and Winston, who slept badly, would lie awake and think aloud, telling her his theories about the Party, about the past, about the proles. She listened with half an ear and corrected him with the shrewdness of the practically minded. She told him about the war — she had no patience with his doubts about whether the enemy was Eurasia or Eastasia this year; everybody knew, she said, that the war was largely a fraud maintained for the disposal of surplus goods and the keeping of fear at the proper temperature, and the rockets that fell on London were probably fired by the Party itself to keep the population frightened. "I once saw a film of a helicopter bombing a raft of refugees," she said. "A woman in the audience laughed. They threw her out. But the bomb was ours. You could tell by the helicopter." Winston marvelled at her: the Party could feed her any lie about history and she would swallow it carelessly, and yet about everything that touched her own life she was clear-eyed as a surgeon.

In the room there was a rat-hole in the wainscot, and once, as they lay talking, a rat thrust its sharp, whiskered snout out of the hole and sniffed the air. Julia flung a shoe at it and it vanished, but Winston had gone cold and rigid in a way out of all proportion to the event, his heart crawling in his chest. He had a horror of rats that lived below reason, in the foundations of him, older than any memory. Julia, seeing his face, held him until the shaking passed, and laughed at him a little, tenderly, not knowing — how could she know — that she had just glimpsed the one door in Winston Smith that had no lock the Party could not pick.

On the dresser stood the paperweight. Julia held it up to the light and admired the coral. "It's a little world," she said, and set it down gently in its place, at the centre of their small, borrowed, doomed apartment of the heart. Above the bed hung St Clement's in its frame. Outside the window the prole woman in the yard below sang as she hung out her washing, a strong, tuneless, endless song, and Winston listened to her and thought: she will be singing in thirty years, when everyone in this room is dust. The proles had stayed human. Whatever happened, the song would go on.

CHAPTER 8
Wine with O'Brien

The invitation, when it came, came exactly as Winston had dreamed it would and in a manner so open that it seemed impossible: in a corridor of the Ministry, O'Brien stopped him, in the hearing of others, and spoke of the Newspeak Dictionary — the Tenth Edition, in which Winston's work had supposedly been noticed — and mentioned, as an afterthought, that a copy of the new edition was at his flat, if Winston cared to call for it one evening. He gave the address aloud. It was a pretext so transparent that it could only be a signal; no member of the Inner Party gave out his address for a dictionary. Winston memorised it the way a man memorises the combination of a safe, and that evening, with Julia beside him — for he had resolved that they would stand or fall together — he went up in the lift of a rich block near the centre of the city, into the other world.

O'Brien's flat was a revelation of luxury so complete that it amounted to a second education. There were thick carpets, white walls, a servant in a white jacket, wine — real wine, a thing Winston had never tasted — in a decanter, and silence: for O'Brien, with a gesture that Winston would remember on the worst day of his life, crossed to the telescreen on the wall and turned it off. Not dimmed. Off. "You can turn it off!" Winston said. "Yes," said O'Brien. "We can turn it off. We have that privilege." Members of the Inner Party could switch off their telescreens, for perhaps half an hour at a time. The two visitors stood in the hush of a room from which the Party's eye had withdrawn, and Winston understood that he had crossed a frontier and there was no way back.

He did not waste the moment. His voice shook at first, but he said what he had come to say: that he and Julia believed there existed a conspiracy, an organisation working against the Party; that they were enemies of the Party, thought-criminals and adulterers; that they wished to join. O'Brien filled their glasses again and questioned them with the calm of a man conducting a business meeting. Would they be prepared to commit murder? Yes. To commit acts of sabotage which might kill hundreds of innocent people? Yes. To throw sulphuric acid in a child's face? Yes. To betray their country to a foreign power? Yes. To lose their identity, to live in disguise, to forge, to steal, to blackmail? Yes, yes, yes — Julia answering beside him, as quick and unflinching as he. To separate, if ordered, and never see each other again? "No!" cried Julia, and Winston, after a heartbeat in which the whole of his life seemed to pass, echoed her: No. O'Brien regarded them with something that might have been respect. "You are prepared to give your lives?" Yes. "You are prepared to commit suicide, if and when we order you to do so?" Yes. "You are prepared, the two of you, to separate and never see one another again?" No — that one, never. It was the only refusal, and O'Brien noted it as a general notes an enemy's one weak position.

He told them little: that the Brotherhood existed; that its leader was Emmanuel Goldstein; that there was a book — the book — written by Goldstein, which every member must read; that Winston would receive it, by an arrangement O'Brien described with great precision, in a briefcase handed to him during Hate Week; that they would receive orders, but no explanation of orders; that they would never know the structure of the organisation, never meet other members beyond their cell, never be thanked, and that if they were caught — as they probably would be — the Brotherhood could do nothing for them. "You will have to live for ever in the midst of a struggle," O'Brien said. "You will never be able to feel that it is growing stronger or the world is growing kinder. If you want a picture of the future—" he paused, and then finished the sentence in the privacy of that silent room with words that Winston would hear again in a place without windows. He gave them wine again before they left, and raised his glass. "To the past," Winston said, on impulse — for the past was his true country. O'Brien's eyes rested on him with a kind of gravity. "The past is more important," he agreed, and they drank. On the stairs, in the lift, in the street, neither of them spoke. They had done it. They had enrolled in their own destruction, deliberately, with open eyes — and for the first time since the diary, Winston felt not fear but a strange, cold elation, as if a weight of years had been set down.

CHAPTER 9
The Book

It came during Hate Week, exactly as promised. The week was a frenzy of preparation — processions, speeches, films, the hanging of enemy effigies, children marching, the whole city wound up to a pitch of patriotic delirium — and in the middle of it, in a crowded room at the Ministry where Winston was helping to organise the demonstrations, a man he did not know stumbled against him, pressed a briefcase into his hand with a muttered apology, and was gone. Inside the briefcase, under some papers, lay a heavy black volume without a title on its cover: the book. The Theory and Practice of Oligarchical Collectivism, by Emmanuel Goldstein. Winston carried it home with the blood singing in his ears, and that evening, in the room over the shop, with Julia beside him in the bed, he began to read aloud.

The book explained the world. It explained it so completely and so calmly that reading it felt like waking from a fever. The world, it said, was divided among three great super-states — Oceania, Eurasia, Eastasia — locked in a war that could never be won and was not meant to be won. The war's true purpose was internal: it consumed the surplus of production, so that the standard of life could be kept low; it preserved the special mental atmosphere a hierarchical society needed; it burned up goods, labour and lives that might otherwise have made the masses too comfortable, and therefore too thoughtful. War was not madness. War was policy. War was peace.

The book explained, too, the shape of every society that had ever existed: the High, who wished to remain where they were; the Middle, who wished to change places with the High; and the Low, who wished, when they had any aim at all, to abolish all distinctions and live as equals. History, it said, was the endless cycling of these three: the Middle would enlist the Low with promises of freedom and justice, overthrow the High, and then, once installed, drive the Low back into servitude and become a new High themselves. The Party alone, of all movements, understood this pattern consciously and had resolved to break it — not by liberating the Low, but by abolishing the cycle: by making its own rule permanent, hereditary in effect if not in name, and immune to challenge from below or decay from within. The proles were left in their ignorance precisely because they were harmless; the danger had never come from them. It came from the Middle — from men like Winston — and so it was the Party's own members who lived under the telescreens, and the proles who were free to sing.

The book explained the Party itself: how every ruling class in history had fallen because it grew soft, or liberal, or unable to adapt, and how the Party had learned from all of them. The Party sought power entirely for its own sake — not wealth, not comfort, not the good of anyone. Its world was a hierarchy maintained for ever, with the High at the top, and its instrument was the control of reality itself. Earlier tyrannies had fallen because they could not prevent men from thinking; the Party had solved this. It did not merely forbid disagreement; it reshaped the mind that might disagree, through Newspeak, which narrowed the words thought could use; through doublethink, the power of holding two contradictory beliefs at once and accepting both; and through the mutability of the past, which removed every standard by which the present might be judged. "The heresy of heresies," Winston read aloud, "was common sense." Julia's breathing had slowed. She was asleep — she had been asleep for some time, curled against him, bored in her bones by abstractions — and Winston read on alone, into the chapter on how the Party perverted every record, every statistic, every memory, and felt the peculiar peace of a man whose worst suspicions have been confirmed by an authority. He was not mad. He was not alone. The sanity he had hidden like a crime was written here, in print, in a book the Brotherhood passed from hand to hand like a sacrament.

One passage he read twice. The book said that the Party's final, most essential command was to reject the evidence of one's eyes and ears; that freedom, if the word was to keep any meaning, was the freedom to say that two plus two make four; and that if this freedom was granted, all else followed. Winston closed the book and lay looking at the ceiling of the little room, at St Clement's on the wall, at Julia asleep, and he thought that he was no longer alone in the most important respect: he knew. The knowledge could be taken from him, he did not doubt that; they could break him and unmake his mind as they had unmade so many. But tonight the knowledge existed in him whole, and the book existed, and somewhere there were others.

"The proles," he said aloud, waking her a little. "It says the future belongs to the proles. They're the only ones who can't be reached. One day they'll wake up." Julia stirred and said something indistinct about the room being cold, and pulled the quilt up, and slept again. Winston set the book carefully on the floor, where the light fell on its black cover, and slept at last himself, more deeply than he had slept in years — slept, with the book in the room and the rhyme unfinished and the rat-hole quiet in the wainscot, like a man who has finished a long journey and does not know he has arrived at the place he was fleeing from.

CHAPTER 10
The Shattering of the Glass

That last night they had talked of the future with the reckless freedom of people who have stopped expecting one. Julia, practical to the end, had spoken of new hiding places, of the country, of ways and means; and Winston had spoken of O'Brien, and of the day when the Brotherhood would send for them in earnest, and neither of them had believed the other, and it had not mattered. They had eaten the last of the real bread. They had drunk the last of the real coffee. Julia had wound the scarlet sash back around her waist over her dress, laughing at the absurdity of it, and then taken it off again because here, in this room, she did not have to wear it. When she slept she slept as she always did, instantly and completely, one arm flung across his chest as though even in sleep she were holding him in the world.

He woke before she did, in the morning light, and lay for a while in the strange peace of the room: the quilt, the engraving, the paperweight on the dresser holding its coral in a drop of glass. "We are the dead," he said aloud, half to himself — a phrase from the book, or from a dream. And from behind the picture on the wall, a voice answered, metallic and precise: "You are the dead." Julia sat up with a strangled cry. The steel engraving of St Clement's fell forward off the wall — it had been hinged — and behind it, set into the plaster, was a telescreen. It had been there all along. It had watched every hour, heard every word, from the first afternoon to the last. There was a crash downstairs, boots on the stair, and the room filled with men in black uniforms, and the first blow knocked Winston to the floor.

They did not hurry. That was part of it: they did not hurry. The men worked methodically, smashing the room as they worked — the dresser overturned, the quilt ripped, the little hoard of real things trampled into the floorboards. Julia was beaten where she lay, efficiently, without anger, and dragged out half-dressed, and Winston, doubled up from a blow to the stomach, watched one of the men pick up the paperweight from the floor and dash it against the heel of his boot. It shattered in a spray of glass across the room, and the coral rolled — such a tiny thing, he saw now, absurdly small, a pink bud of a thing — and came to rest against the leg of the dresser. So that was the size of the world they had built. He had time to understand it fully before the next blow fell.

Then Mr Charrington came into the room, and he was not Mr Charrington. The mild eyes were the same, but the stoop was gone, the vagueness was gone, the years seemed gone; he stood straight in the wreckage in the black uniform of the Thought Police, younger and harder, a man who had played a part for years with perfect patience — had perhaps kept that shop, and that room, and that rhyme, as a trap is kept, baited with the past, for people exactly like Winston. "Oranges and lemons," Winston thought, with a clarity that felt like falling, "say the bells of St Clement's. You gave me the whole rhyme once, and I never asked how it ended." He remembered now that Charrington had known another line, and had sung it for him on a later visit, smiling: "Here comes a chopper to chop off your head." It had seemed quaint. Everything had seemed quaint. That was the design of the trap.

They were taken separately. Winston was marched out through the shop, past the dusty window where he had first seen the paperweight, into a street that looked exactly as it had always looked — the prole woman was singing somewhere, two streets away, and her song reached him as he was pushed into the van, strong and tuneless and indifferent, the sound of the world that would outlive him. He had written in his diary that if there was hope it lay in the proles. The van door closed on the song.

In the van, handcuffed, jolted through streets he could not see, he found his mind working with a terrible, useless clarity, the way it had worked in the alcove on the first day. He thought of the diary, which they would have found by now, and of the photograph he had destroyed years ago, and of O'Brien's spectacles flashing in the Hate. He thought: they have known everything, from the beginning, perhaps from the first word I wrote. The trap had not been built for him in a week or a month; it had the patience of an institution in it, the long calm of people who could afford to let a man ripen for years in his crime, feeding on hope, growing tender and careless, until he was ready to be picked. He wondered whether Julia was in another van, on another street, thinking the same thoughts. He wondered whether she would forgive him for the room — for it had been his room, his shop, his paperweight, his discovery, and he had led her into it as proudly as a bridegroom. The van turned a corner. Somewhere ahead of them, windowless and white, the Ministry of Love was waiting with its lights on, as it waited at every hour of the day and night, for everyone. He understood, with the part of his mind that was still working, that hope had never been the point: the point was that the proles would never know his name, and the room was smashed, and Julia was gone into the machine, and he was going where there were no windows and the lights were never turned off. He was going to the Ministry of Love.

CHAPTER 11
The Ministry of Love

The Ministry of Love had no windows. It was a place of white tiles and white light, of corridors that smelled of steel and disinfectant, of doors without handles and cells where the lights burned day and night and four telescreens watched from the four walls. Winston was thrown into a cell already crowded with prisoners, and there his education in the true nature of his country began — not from books this time, but from the procession of the damned who passed through that room.

He learned the order of things quickly. There were the common criminals first — thieves, black-marketeers, prostitutes, murderers — who feared the police but did not fear the room, who shouted, joked, fought over food and were dragged out and back with a rough, almost familiar violence; and then there were the politicals, who sat in silence, who did not eat when food came, who watched the door. A woman prisoner, arrested for some offence against chastity, tried to sit beside Winston and offer him food, and was driven off by the guards with blows — no one was permitted any small mercy. Food, he understood, was a rumour. Hunger became the fixed centre of his existence, a pain so constant that it ceased to be pain and became the medium he lived in, like water to a fish. He dreamed of food. All the prisoners dreamed of food.

Some he knew. Ampleforth, the poet from his own department — a mild, bewildered man whose crime had been to leave the word "God" at the end of a line of Kipling he was versifying, because no rhyme could replace it — sat waiting to learn what would be done to him, and was philosophical about it in a way that broke Winston's heart: the poem, Ampleforth explained, had simply required the word. And Parsons — Parsons, his neighbour, the sweating, stupid, loyal organiser of every collection and hike and spying expedition, the last man in Oceania Winston would have suspected — was thrust into the cell one day in a state of shattered pride, denounced, it emerged, by his own small daughter, who had listened at a door and heard him say "Down with Big Brother" in his sleep. The terrible thing was that Parsons was proud of the child. "She's a clever little thing," he kept saying. "It shows the spirit's there. The Party's in her blood." He was awaiting his fate almost gratefully, and Winston, watching him, understood that the machine did not even require its victims to understand their crime; it preferred them grateful.

From the cell, prisoners were taken one by one through a door, and some came back changed in ways that could not be described, and some did not come back. Winston was beaten — casually at first, by guards, with fists and boots and truncheons, in the corridor and in a smaller room — and the beatings taught him the first lesson of the place: that the body was a traitor that would sign anything. He confessed to everything they suggested: to espionage, sabotage, murder, to membership of the Brotherhood, to crimes committed and crimes impossible. Everyone confessed. Confession was not the point; it was merely the admission price. In his cell between beatings he lay on the board bed and thought of Julia and did not know if she was alive, and thought of O'Brien — for O'Brien would surely be arrested too, O'Brien was of the Brotherhood, O'Brien would be brought here — and the thought was, absurdly, a comfort to him, the last warmth in the cold building: that somewhere in this white hell, the one man who understood him was also suffering, and they were, in that sense, still on the same side.

Then, one day, the door of the cell opened and O'Brien walked in. Winston struggled up from the bench with joy breaking over him like light. "They've got you too!" he cried. O'Brien looked at him with the calm of a man correcting a child's error in arithmetic. "They got me a long time ago," he said. And the guard behind him, at a small signal, brought his truncheon down across Winston's elbow, and the world exploded into a pain so total that for a moment Winston could not even scream, and in the white fire of it he understood everything: that there had never been a Brotherhood for him, never a book that was not bait, never a friend. There had only been O'Brien, waiting, patient as geology, on the other side of the trap — O'Brien the priest of power, who had played his part for seven years, who had drawn him out with a look across a crowded room, and who now stood over him in the place where the lights never went out, ready to begin the real work at last.

CHAPTER 12
The Lessons of Power

The work had three stages, O'Brien explained, as a doctor might explain a cure: learning, understanding, acceptance. Winston was strapped, at times, to a machine with a dial, and O'Brien's hand rested on a lever, and pain could be administered in graduated amounts, like light through a dimmer, up to a maximum that was not death — death, Winston learned, was not available; they would not let him have it. Pain was the tutor. Under its instruction he was led, step by step, through the demolition of his inner world.

First, the facts he had hoarded like contraband. The photograph of Jones, Aaronson and Rutherford — the one piece of evidence he had ever held — O'Brien produced it himself, held it for a moment above the memory hole in the wall of the torture room, and let it fall. It had existed. Now it did not exist, and in a little while, O'Brien explained, it would never have existed; and Winston, who had already begun to find that his memory obligingly blurred and shifted under pressure, felt the ground of the past go soft beneath him. "You are a flaw in the pattern, Winston," O'Brien said. "You believe that reality is something objective, external, existing in its own right. But I tell you, Winston, that reality exists only in the human mind, and nowhere else — and not in the individual mind, which makes mistakes and perishes, but in the mind of the Party, which is collective and immortal. Whatever the Party holds to be truth is truth. It is impossible to see reality except by looking through the eyes of the Party."

Then the arithmetic. O'Brien held up four fingers of his hand. How many fingers, Winston? Four, said Winston, desperately — and the dial climbed, and the pain became a universe, and still, with the last of himself, he said four; and the dial climbed again, until there were not four fingers and not five fingers but a shifting agony in which fingers had no number; and at last, sobbing, he begged to know the answer, any answer, whatever O'Brien wished. Five, if the Party said five. But the lesson was subtler than lying to stop the pain. O'Brien did not want a lie. He wanted Winston to see five — to be cured of the madness of trusting his own eyes. "Sometimes, Winston, they are five. Sometimes they are three. Sometimes they are all of them at once. You must try harder. It is not easy to become sane."

And then the question beneath all questions, the one Winston himself had once asked in the diary of his mind: why? Why did the Party want this? Not for the future, not for posterity, not for any end beyond itself. O'Brien answered with a candour more frightening than any cruelty. The Party sought power entirely for its own sake. Power was not a means; it was an end. The object of torture was torture. The object of power was power. If Winston wanted a picture of the future, O'Brien said — and Winston recognised, with a sensation like drowning, the words that had been left unfinished in the silent flat, over the wine — imagine a boot stamping on a human face, for ever. "And remember," O'Brien said, almost gently, "that it is for ever. The face will always be there to be stamped upon. And you, Winston — you will be cured. We do not destroy the heretic because he resists us. We capture his inner mind. We make the brain perfect before we blow it out. When finally you surrender to us, it must be of your own free will." Through all of it there ran a hideous intimacy. O'Brien understood him as no one ever had; O'Brien anticipated his thoughts, finished his arguments, cradled his head when the convulsions passed, wiped his face. Torturer, protector, inquisitor and friend were one person, and Winston, starved, broken, weeping, began to love him for it — began to feel that O'Brien alone stood between him and the darkness, that the hand on the lever was also the hand that fed him, that understanding him at last was worth any price. His body healed between sessions; they fed him, let him sleep, gave him back his strength with a terrible tenderness, because the work required him whole. He put on weight. He exercised in his cell. He practised doublethink like scales on a piano — two plus two are five, two plus two are three, the past is whatever is required — and felt his mind grow supple and quick in its new sickness. He was nearly cured. He knew it himself. There remained only one thing, one last room in him where something still lived that had not surrendered; and O'Brien, who knew him better than he knew himself, knew exactly where it was, and had saved it for the end.

CHAPTER 13
Room 101

"There is one thing I have not asked you," O'Brien said, in the calm that always came before the worst of it. They had been speaking of Julia. Winston had confessed everything about her — every meeting, every word, every act; he had described her to the interrogators in detail until there was nothing left of their love that he had not handed over. And yet O'Brien was dissatisfied, because O'Brien was looking for something deeper than information. "You have not betrayed Julia," he said. "Not in the way that matters. When you were arrested you stopped loving her — oh, in your heart, perhaps, for a while. But since then there has been no time when you wished the world to treat her worse than yourself. You still think of her as someone you would protect. That is the last human feeling left in you, Winston, and we cannot leave it there."

Winston begged, of course. He had begged before, for lesser things, and been refused with the same mild attention; but this time he begged as he had never begged in the Ministry, with a fluency that astonished him — offering confessions he had not yet made, betrayals he had not yet committed, names, dates, anything, everything, in exchange for not being taken down that corridor. O'Brien listened to all of it the way a priest listens to a confession he has heard a hundred times, and when Winston had exhausted himself, O'Brien shook his head almost sadly. It was not a question of information. It had never been a question of information. "We are not interested in what you can tell us, Winston. We are interested in what you are. And what you are is not yet finished." He rose, and the guards rose with him, and the long walk began — past cells from which sounds came that Winston had learned not to interpret, down stairs he had never been allowed to climb, into the deep places of the building where even the telescreens seemed to watch with a different quality of attention.

He explained about Room 101 as a doctor explains an operation that cannot be avoided. In Room 101, there was, for every prisoner, the worst thing in the world. It varied from person to person: it might be burial alive, or death by fire, or drowning, or impalement, or fifty other deaths; sometimes it was a trivial thing, not even fatal. The worst thing in the world, for Winston Smith, had been known to the Ministry for a long time — perhaps it had always been known; perhaps it was written in him in a script older than memory. He had shown it once, in a warm room, when a snout had come out of a hole in the wainscot and he had gone rigid in a woman's arms. Rats. They took him down a long corridor to a room he had never seen, and they strapped him in a chair, and they fitted over his head a mask — a cage, shaped like a fencing mask — and in the cage, divided from his face by a wire partition that could be raised by a lever, there were two rats. Great rats, bold rats, rats from the sewers, hungry; he could hear them scrabbling, could smell them, could see their eyes. "They will leap onto your face," O'Brien said quietly, from somewhere behind him, "and bore straight through it. Sometimes they attack the eyes first. Sometimes they burrow through the cheeks and devour the tongue."

Winston screamed. He screamed for the lever not to be touched; he screamed explanations, confessions, promises; he screamed things he did not know he knew. The partition had not yet moved. The rats, sensing the feast, hurled themselves against the wire. And in that extremity, at the very bottom of himself, below the level of thought, Winston found the one thing that could save him — the only thing, and he seized it with the strength of a drowning man seizing a spar. There was one person onto whom he could transfer the punishment, one person who could interpose her body between himself and the rats, and he did not merely think it, he willed it, with his whole soul, aloud, again and again, until the screaming was the whole of him: "Do it to Julia! Do it to Julia! Not me! Julia! I don't care what you do to her. Tear her face off, strip her to the bones. Not me! Julia! Not me!"

The lever was never pulled. The cage was taken away. He had done it: he had betrayed her — not with words, which are cheap and can be extracted from anyone, but in the only sense that finally mattered; in the moment of extremity he had wished her in his place, and meant it. Something had snapped inside him, cleanly and for ever, like a bone that would never set. O'Brien watched him with the satisfaction of a craftsman whose work is complete. The cure was finished. What remained of Winston Smith was released in due course — fattened a little more, given new teeth, given a small job of no importance, given his freedom, such as it was. He walked out of the Ministry of Love into the grey city, a man who had been hollowed out and filled up again with something else, and there was nothing left in the world that he was capable of betraying, because there was nothing left in the world that he loved more than himself — except, as it would turn out, one thing.

CHAPTER 14
The Chestnut Tree Café

The Chestnut Tree Café was where the released went — the cured, the emptied, the ghosts of the Ministry's graduating class. Disgraced chess-players, purged officials awaiting rearrest, broken men nursing glasses of gin at marble-topped tables while the telescreen played music and the waiters, who knew everything, pretended to know nothing. Winston sat there most afternoons now, at his usual table, with a chess problem open in The Times that he never solved and a glass of Victory Gin that the waiter refilled, unasked, whenever it emptied — a perquisite of his condition, like a pension. He had a job, of sorts, a sinecure on a sub-committee; he had money enough; he had his health, after a fashion. His body had been repaired. It was only everything else that had been taken.

His days had a shape now, of a kind. He rose in his new flat — better than the old one; the Ministry looked after its graduates — and went to his committee, where he sat through meetings about matters of no consequence and occasionally spoke, and was listened to with the respect due to a man who had been through the mill and come out the other side. Sometimes, in the middle of a meeting, he would be visited by a brief, vivid episode — a memory that was not quite a memory, of a room, a song, a woman's arm across his chest — and a spasm of something would cross his face, and he would reach, when he could, for the gin, and the gin would smooth it away. The doctors had told him gin was bad for him. The waiter at the Chestnut Tree had evidently not been told, or had been told the opposite. Between the committee and the café, between the café and his bed, Winston's life moved like a tram on rails laid down by other people, and he was grateful for the rails. Thinking had been the disease. He had been cured of it, and the cure, he would have told anyone who asked — and no one asked — had been worth everything it cost.

Julia came to the café once — or rather, he encountered her, on a bitter day in the park nearby, where the released sometimes walked; and they spoke, because there was no longer any reason not to speak, which was itself the measure of what had been done to them. She was changed. Her body had thickened and stiffened, her face was coarsened, and there was a long scar across her forehead and temple that her hair did not quite hide. She looked at him with the flat curiosity of a person examining a stranger's photograph. "I betrayed you," she said, in a matter-of-fact voice. "Sometimes," Winston said, "sometimes they threaten you with something — something you can't stand up to, can't even think about. And then you say: don't do it to me, do it to somebody else, do it to So-and-so. And afterwards you don't feel the same towards that person again." "No," she said. "You don't." She told him, in the same flat voice, the practical facts: she had been given work, she was well enough, she did not read books any more, she went to the meetings. He told her his own facts, which were the same facts. It was like two clerks comparing inventories of a warehouse that had burned down. Once, reaching for a match, her hand brushed his, and both of them drew back — not in disgust, not in fear, but with the mild, automatic recoil of people who have been taught, in the deepest part of themselves, that this particular door opens onto nothing. She said she supposed they would not meet again. He said he supposed not. Neither of them suggested otherwise. They stood a while in the cold without anything further to say, and parted without touching, without regret, almost without interest — two people who had once, in a room over a junk shop, held the whole of the past and the future between them, and who now could not summon enough feeling to be embarrassed. That was the triumph of the Ministry of Love: not that they had been punished, but that the thing between them, the realest thing in either of their lives, had been killed so thoroughly that neither of them even mourned it.

Back in the café, the gin was warm in him and the telescreen was suddenly loud with a special bulletin. Oceania had won a great victory in Africa; the news crashed over the little room in waves of trumpets, and the patrons cheered, and Winston felt the cheering rise in his own chest like a tide returning to a shore. He looked up at the enormous face on the screen — the face he had once cursed in a diary, in an alcove, in another life — and two gin-scented tears trickled down the sides of his nose. But it was all right, everything was all right, the struggle was finished. He gazed up at the huge face with a serenity he had never known in all his years of rebellion, and understood at last that he had misunderstood everything: that the long, wasted years of hatred had been a sickness, and the Ministry had been his hospital, and O'Brien his physician, and the pain the price of being made whole. He had won the victory over himself. He loved Big Brother.
