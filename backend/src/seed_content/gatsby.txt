The Great Gatsby
F. Scott Fitzgerald

CHAPTER 1
A Cottage in West Egg

In the spring of 1922 I came East to learn the bond business, and because a man I knew in the army had spoken well of Long Island, I rented a house in the village of West Egg. It was a small weather-beaten cottage squeezed between two enormous mansions, an eyesore to my wealthy neighbors, but a bargain at eighty dollars a month. From my little lawn I could see a strip of the Sound, and on either side of me the great houses rose like the palaces of men who had made fortunes too quickly to know what to do with them.

My own family, the Carraways, had been prominent in the Middle West for three generations. My father ran a wholesale hardware business, and before I left he gave me a piece of advice that has stayed with me ever since. Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone, he told me, just remember that all the people in this world haven't had the advantages that you've had. I have tried to live by it, and it has made me slow to judge the curious, restless people who crossed my path that summer — though it did not make me blind to what they were.

Across the bay, in the more fashionable East Egg, lived my cousin Daisy Buchanan, whom I had known slightly at college, and her husband Tom, who had been a football star at New Haven and had come into a fortune so large it seemed to excuse him from ordinary behavior. I had not seen either of them since the war, and when Tom telephoned to invite me to dinner, I accepted out of a vague feeling that family ties ought to mean something.

The Buchanan house was a Georgian Colonial mansion of red and white brick, with a lawn that began at the beach and ran a quarter of a mile to the front door, jumping over sundials and garden paths as if it could not be contained. Tom met me on the porch in riding clothes, and I was struck again by his sheer physical presence — a tall, hard-muscled man of thirty with a cruel, arrogant body and a voice full of husky contempt for nearly everything.

Inside, the French windows stood open to the evening, and the curtains blew in and out like pale flags. Daisy and her friend Jordan Baker lay stretched on an enormous couch, both in white dresses that rippled as if they had just come in from a short flight around the house. Jordan was a slim, golden-armed girl with a face of perpetual, faint boredom; she was a golf champion, and her photograph was in all the papers. Daisy laughed her thrilling, artificial laugh and said she was paralyzed with happiness to see me.

Dinner was not a comfortable meal. Tom, between courses, began to lecture us about a book he had been reading, something about the rise of the colored empires and the decline of the white race, and he spoke with such gloomy conviction that Daisy yawned openly and changed the subject. Twice the telephone rang for Tom, and twice he left the room to take the call in the library. Jordan, watching my face, leaned close and whispered that Tom had a woman in New York. I did not know what to say to that. The second time he went out, Daisy followed him, and Jordan and I sat in an embarrassed silence broken only by the sound of low, furious voices from the other room.

When they came back, the evening limped on until it was time for me to go. Daisy walked me to the door and, in the darkness, spoke for a moment with a strange intensity about her little daughter, saying she hoped the child would be a fool — that it was the best thing a girl could be in this world, a beautiful little fool. I drove home unsettled, feeling that the Buchanans' glittering world was rotten somewhere under the surface, and that I had been allowed to glimpse the rot.

My cottage, when I reached it, was dark, but the mansion next door was ablaze with light. As I stood on my lawn I saw a figure come out of the side door and stand for a moment in the moonlight. It was my neighbor, the mysterious Mr. Gatsby, of whom I had heard so many rumors and knew nothing at all. He stood with his hands in his pockets, looking out across the dark water. Then, slowly, he stretched out his arms toward the bay, toward a single green light at the end of a dock far across the Sound. His arms trembled. I looked out at the light myself, and when I turned back, he was gone, and I was alone again in the unquiet darkness, wondering what a man could want so much that he reached for it across an entire bay of black water.

CHAPTER 2
Dinner at East Egg

Although I have already described that first dinner as I remember it, it deserves a second telling, for the evening set the pattern of everything that followed. The Buchanans did nothing by halves. Their wealth was not merely money; it was a kind of restlessness, a drifting from place to place and person to person, spending and discarding as they went, and letting other people clean up the messes they made.

Tom Buchanan had been one of the most powerful ends who ever played football at New Haven, and he had never quite recovered from the fact that his greatest triumph was behind him at twenty-one. Since then he had been trying, with increasing violence, to recapture that feeling of mastery — in polo ponies, in motorcars, in other men's wives and his own. There was a quality of permanent resentment about him, as if the world owed him a dominance it no longer quite granted, and his conversation was a series of assertions meant to prove that he was still the man in charge of every room he entered.

Daisy was different, and harder to know. Her voice was the first thing anyone noticed — a low, thrilling voice that seemed to promise delightful things in the near future, a voice full of money, as I would later understand. She had a way of leaning toward you and murmuring as if you alone were being told a wonderful secret, and then forgetting the secret entirely a moment later. People adored her, and she received their adoration the way a flower receives rain, without effort and without much gratitude. Beneath the charm there was a sadness she could not name, the sadness of a woman who had been given everything except a reason to care about any of it.

Jordan Baker intrigued me from the start. She was incurably dishonest — she had once left a borrowed car out in the rain with the top down and lied about it, and there was a murmur about her first golf tournament, a suggestion that she had moved her ball — but her dishonesty had a cool, careless quality that I found almost attractive. She kept herself balanced at the very edge of propriety, like her own posture, which was like that of a young cadet. She liked to suggest that she was too clever to be caught and too bored to be good.

That evening at the table, I watched the three of them and felt myself to be the only honest person in the room, which was not a comfortable feeling. Tom's mistress telephoned during dinner, openly, as if it were the most natural thing in the world. Daisy, when she followed him from the room, was not fighting for her marriage; she was fighting for her pride, and there is a difference. When she came back she was bright and brittle, and she told me a long story about the butler's nose, laughing too hard at her own joke.

Later, on the porch, she spoke of her daughter's birth. She said that when the nurse told her the baby was a girl, she had wept, and said she hoped the child would be a fool. It was the most honest thing she said all night, and it told me more about her life with Tom than any scandal could have done. She had learned, in three years of marriage, that intelligence in a woman was only a sharper instrument with which to feel pain.

I left East Egg that night with the sense that I had been admitted, briefly and accidentally, into a world that ran on rules I did not understand. I was an insider now, by blood and by dinner invitation, and yet I was also an outsider, watching. That double position — within and without, enchanted and repelled — was to be my place for the whole of that summer, and everything I saw, I saw from the doorway.

Jordan drove me part of the way home in her car, and for a while neither of us spoke. She was not a woman troubled by other people's marriages, and she regarded the whole evening with the amused detachment of a spectator at a sport in which she had no stake. "They're a rotten crowd," she said at last, quite cheerfully, as if delivering the score of a match. "You're worth the whole damn bunch put together." It was the only compliment I ever heard her pay anyone, and I have thought of it often since — not because I believed it, exactly, but because it showed me that she, at least, had been watching the same evening I had, and had drawn her own conclusions. We parted at my gate without ceremony. Above my cottage the lights of my neighbor's mansion were burning as they burned every night, though no party was in progress, and I remember wondering, for the first time, whether all that light was lit for guests at all — or whether it was a signal, burning night after night, for an audience of one across the bay.

CHAPTER 3
The Valley of Ashes

About halfway between West Egg and New York, the motor road hastily joins the railroad and runs beside it for a quarter of a mile, so as to shrink away from a desolate stretch of land. This is a valley of ashes — a gray, fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens, where ash-gray men swarm up with leaden spades and stir up an impenetrable cloud of dust. Above it all, the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg brood on enormous yellow spectacles from a fading billboard, the relic of some oculist's forgotten advertisement, gazing down on the dumping ground with a stare that never sleeps.

It was to this dismal place that Tom Buchanan took me one afternoon, braking the car suddenly in front of a small garage. He had told me, with the proprietary air of a man showing off a possession, that he wanted me to meet his girl. The garage belonged to George Wilson, a blond, spiritless man, anaemic and faintly handsome, whose whole manner spoke of a life spent waiting for something that never came. His business was repair work and the sale of cars, and it was plainly not prospering. When Tom strode in, Wilson brightened with a servile hope — Tom had promised to sell him a car, and Wilson clung to the promise the way a drowning man clings to a rope.

Wilson's wife Myrtle came down the stairs from the apartment above the garage, and she was everything her husband was not. She was a stout, sensuous woman in her middle thirties, with a vitality that filled the room; she carried her flesh sensuously, as women of appetite do, and her face contained no facet or gleam of beauty, but an immediately perceptible vitality, an indefinable force, as if the nerves of her body were continually smouldering. She looked at Tom with undisguised longing and at her husband with open contempt, and when Tom told her to take the next train to the city, she agreed with a briskness that needed no explanation between them.

In New York, Tom had taken a small apartment on 158th Street, in one of a long row of yellow brick apartment buildings, and it was there that the three of us went that afternoon. The living room was crowded with tapestried furniture entirely too large for it, so that to move about was to stumble continually over scenes of ladies swinging in the gardens of Versailles. Myrtle, once installed, underwent a transformation. She changed her dress for an afternoon frock of cream-colored chiffon, and with the change her personality expanded: she became affected, haughty, and intensely artificial, speaking of clothes, of servants, of the inferior people she intended to cut, as if she were rehearsing for a part in a play about a rich woman.

Other guests arrived — Myrtle's sister Catherine, a slender, worldly girl of about thirty, and the McKees, a photographer and his pale, shrill wife, who lived in the flat below. The whisky came out, and then more whisky, and the afternoon dissolved into that peculiar intensity of a party held in too small a room, where everyone talks louder and laughs harder than the occasion warrants. Catherine told me, in confidence, that neither Tom nor Myrtle could stand the person they were married to, and that it was a pity they couldn't simply run away together. "It's really his wife that's keeping them apart," she confided. "She's a Catholic, and they don't believe in divorce." Daisy was not a Catholic, and I was a little shocked at the elaborateness of the lie.

As the evening wore on, Myrtle grew louder and more expansive, describing her first meeting with Tom — how he had pressed against her in a crowded railway car, in a suit so fine she could not keep her eyes off it, and how she had known at once that he was a gentleman. Then, emboldened by drink, she began to chant Daisy's name, over and over, taunting him with it: "Daisy! Daisy! Daisy! I'll say it whenever I want to! Daisy! Dai—" Tom, without rising, made a short deft movement with his open hand and broke her nose with one savage blow. There was a hush, then towels and cold water and sobbing, and the party ended the way such parties do — suddenly, in blood and confusion, with the McKees fumbling for their coats and me following them down the stairs into the night, half-drunk and thoroughly sickened, waiting for the four o'clock train in the cold Pennsylvania Station.

On the train I sat across from a man reading a newspaper and tried to make sense of the afternoon. I had been taken up, for a few hours, into the machinery of Tom Buchanan's other life, and what stayed with me was not the violence — though the sound of that blow, and Myrtle's muffled sobbing through the towels, would stay with me a long time — but the casualness of it. Tom had shown me his mistress the way a man shows off a horse or a boat: as a possession, and as a proof. And I, who had gone along and drunk his whisky and kept his secret, was in some small way already implicated. That is how such worlds recruit you: not by asking you to do anything wicked, but by asking you, again and again, merely to watch.

CHAPTER 4
An Invitation

By the middle of that summer, my neighbor's name had become a kind of currency in New York. Gatsby. People used it the way they used the name of a legend — to explain anything mysterious, to season any dull conversation. He had killed a man once, some said. He had been a German spy during the war. He was a bootlegger; he was a cousin of the Kaiser; he had once been an Oxford man; he had emerged, fully formed and impossibly rich, from nowhere at all. His enormous parties were famous from one end of Long Island to the other, and yet almost no one who attended them had ever met the host.

I went to his house first as one of the few people who were actually invited. A chauffeur in a uniform of robin's-egg blue crossed my lawn one morning with a card: Mr. Gatsby requested the honor of my presence at one of his little parties that night. I dressed that evening with a strange sense of ceremony and walked the short distance to a mansion transformed. The gardens glowed with a thousand lights. A full orchestra played in the hall. In the marble swimming pool and along the beach, guests in evening clothes drifted like moths among the whisperings and the champagne and the stars. There were bars where the gin flowed without ceasing, tables laden with spiced baked hams and salads of harlequin design, pastry pigs and turkeys bewitched to a dark gold. Rolls Royce cars ferried guests from the station. It was less a party than a small, self-contained world of pleasure, humming on its own axis.

I knew no one. I drank two cocktails, looked for my host, and found instead Jordan Baker, who had come with a crowd from East Egg. Together we explored the house, and in the Gothic library we came upon a small, stout, middle-aged man with enormous owl-eyed spectacles, very drunk and very earnest, who seized upon the books as a miracle. "They're real," he informed us solemnly. "See? Absolutely real — have pages and everything. I thought they'd be a nice durable cardboard." He explained that he had expected the library to be a stage set, but that Gatsby was a genuine believer in the printed word: he had bought real books and never cut the pages. "What thoroughness! What realism!" he kept saying. "Knew when to stop, too — didn't cut the pages."

Later, in the garden, I fell into conversation with a man of about my own age, a fellow veteran, and we spoke of the war and of France. When someone at our table remarked that Gatsby would not tell anyone anything about himself, my companion smiled. "I beg your pardon," he said. "I'm Gatsby." I was startled into an apology; I had been talking with my host for half an hour without knowing it. He was a man of about thirty, with a tanned skin, close-cropped hair, and an easy, elegant manner that somehow combined formality with warmth. When he smiled, it was one of those rare smiles with a quality of eternal reassurance in it, that you may come across four or five times in a life. It understood you just as far as you wanted to be understood, believed in you as you would like to believe in yourself.

He told me, almost apologetically, that he had noticed my house and hoped I had enjoyed myself, and that he would like me to take a ride in his hydroplane the next morning. Then an attendant came with a message, and he excused himself with perfect courtesy. A moment later I heard two women behind me gossiping: he was a nephew of von Hindenburg, he was a murderer, he had a whole chain of drugstores. Jordan, beside me, smiled her bored smile. "I've just heard the most amazing thing," she said. But the party was breaking up in confusion — a car had gone into the ditch outside the gates, a wheel torn off, and the owl-eyed man stood in the road insisting, to anyone who would listen, that he had not been driving, that he knew nothing about mechanics, and that the whole thing was a miracle of modern motoring. I walked home through the dawn with my head full of music and rumor, no wiser about my neighbor than when I had come.

And yet something had shifted. Until that night, Gatsby had been a name, a house, a blaze of light next door. Now he was a man who smiled as if he had been waiting his whole life to be pleased to meet you, who apologized for his own party, who vanished at intervals to answer telephones with the gravity of a general receiving dispatches from a front no one else could see. Jordan, before we parted, had promised me something: "I've just heard the most amazing thing, and here I am, dying to tell you about it." She would tell me at tea, she said, in a day or two. I did not yet know that the amazing thing concerned Daisy, and my cousin's marriage, and a five-year-old love affair that had never ended. I only knew that I had been taken, without being consulted, one step deeper into the confidence of that strange, glittering world — and that I did not altogether dislike it.

CHAPTER 5
Lunch in the City

A few days after the party, Gatsby called for me in the morning in a car that defied description — a huge, cream-colored circus wagon of a motorcar, with a labyrinth of windshields and a horn that played three notes. As we drove toward the city he began, with a strange formality, to tell me his history. "I don't want you to get a wrong idea of me from all these stories you hear," he said. He claimed to be the son of wealthy people in the Middle West — San Francisco, he said, when I asked — that his family were all dead, that he had been educated at Oxford, and that he had lived in all the capitals of Europe, collecting jewels and hunting big game, before serving with distinction in the war. He produced, as evidence, a photograph of himself in Oxford dress standing among other young men, and a medal from Montenegro, inscribed with his name and the words of a grateful republic. I listened with growing disbelief, and yet there was something in his manner — a kind of desperate sincerity — that made me reluctant to laugh. He wanted to be believed the way other men want to be loved.

In the city we lunched at a dim restaurant on Forty-second Street with a friend of Gatsby's, a Mr. Meyer Wolfsheim, a small, flat-nosed Jew with a large head and two fine growths of hair tumbling from his nostrils. His cuff buttons, I noticed with a start, were made of human molars. Wolfsheim spoke admiringly of Gatsby — "a gentleman, a man of fine breeding, an Oggsford man" — and then, in an undertone, revealed that he himself was the man who had fixed the World's Series in 1919. He said it as casually as if he were discussing the weather. When I expressed astonishment that one man could play with the faith of fifty million people, he shrugged: "They can't get him, old sport. He's smart." After lunch, Wolfsheim rose, shook my hand, and remarked that Gatsby would never so much as look at a business friend's wife. The remark hung in the air long after he had gone, and I began to understand that my neighbor moved in a world of fixers and shadows, however golden its surface.

That afternoon, at tea, Jordan Baker told me the story I had been waiting to hear without knowing it. She had known Daisy Fay in Louisville in 1917, when Daisy was eighteen and the most popular girl in the city, courted by a procession of officers. One of them was a young lieutenant named Jay Gatsby, and Jordan had seen them together, so absorbed in each other that the rest of the world had plainly ceased to exist for them. Gatsby had gone overseas; Daisy, after a period of frantic gaiety, had married Tom Buchanan in a wedding of almost royal splendor — Tom had given her a string of pearls valued at three hundred and fifty thousand dollars. But on the night before the wedding, Jordan had found her drunk for the first time in her life, clutching a letter, weeping in her bath, insisting the wedding be called off. The next day she married Tom without so much as a shiver, and sailed away on a wedding trip, and the matter was closed.

Gatsby had come back from the war to find her married and gone. He had bought his great house in West Egg for one reason only: it stood directly across the bay from hers. "Gatsby bought that house so that Daisy would be just across the dock," Jordan said. The green light I had seen him reach for on my first night was the light at the end of Daisy's dock. He had thrown his parties, Jordan believed, in the persistent hope that she might wander into one of them by chance. She had never come. Now, through Jordan, he was making a request: would I invite Daisy to tea at my cottage, some afternoon, and let him come over? He did not want to force his way into her house through her husband. He wanted the meeting to seem accidental, and he wanted it in my house because mine was the one neutral ground between them.

I agreed, though I did not entirely understand what I was agreeing to. That night I lay awake thinking of the strange geometry of it — five years of wealth, of parties, of reaching across black water, all converging on a cup of tea in my eighty-dollar cottage.

I thought, too, about what it must have cost him to ask. Gatsby did nothing by halves and asked for nothing small; but this request, routed through Jordan and then through me, had the humility of a man who understood that the thing he wanted most could not be seized, only permitted. He had commanded armies of servants, bought a mansion, filled it with light and music for a whole summer of strangers — and in the end he was reduced, like any shy boy in Louisville, to asking a friend of a friend whether the girl might possibly come to tea. There was something magnificent in the scale of his hope, and something terrible in its fragility. I resolved to do the thing well, and to ask no questions.

CHAPTER 6
The Reunion

I chose a day when I knew Tom would be away and telephoned Daisy, inviting her to tea and asking her, with an awkwardness that made me blush, not to bring her husband. She seemed puzzled but agreed. Gatsby, informed of the plan, descended into a frenzy of preparation. He sent a man to mow my lawn. He arrived on the day itself hours early, pale and sleepless, with a haunted look in his eyes, and paced my small living room insisting that everything was wrong: the house was too small, the day too wet, the whole scheme a failure. When Daisy's car finally came down the drive, he panicked and bolted out the back, only to reappear at the front door five minutes later, soaked to the skin, knocking with a studied casualness that deceived no one.

The first half hour was agony. Gatsby stood stiffly by the mantel, his face white, answering Daisy's remarks in monosyllables while the clock ticked and the rain drummed on the roof. Daisy, for her part, was embarrassed and uncertain; she had not known what to expect, and the tall, silent stranger seemed to have nothing to do with the officer she had loved. I made some excuse and left them alone in the kitchen's doorway. When I returned, the room had changed as completely as if a light had been switched on. Gatsby was sitting on the couch, and he was glowing — literally glowing, with a radiance that filled the small room. Daisy stood before the window, and her face was wet with tears of happiness. "It's so long," she kept saying. "It's so very long since we met." They had forgotten I existed. In that moment I understood that for Gatsby this was not the beginning of anything. It was the recovery of something he had been reliving, in perfect detail, for five years.

Gatsby insisted on showing us his house, and we walked through the wet grass to the mansion. He led us through music rooms and libraries and bathrooms with gold taps, through bedrooms where a man in a dressing gown was still asleep at four in the afternoon — "the hairdresser," Gatsby explained vaguely. Daisy admired everything with genuine delight, bending her head over the piles of silk pajamas and the rows of suits. Then Gatsby began to throw his shirts onto the table before her — shirts of sheer linen and thick silk and fine flannel, in stripes and scrolls and plaids, in coral and apple-green and lavender and faint orange — until the pile rose like a soft mountain on the table. Daisy pressed her face into the shirts and began to cry. "They're such beautiful shirts," she sobbed. "It makes me sad because I've never seen such — such beautiful shirts before." It was the shirts, of course, and it was everything the shirts stood for: the five years, the wealth, the life she might have had.

Standing in that room, I watched Gatsby watch her, and I saw his wonder at her presence slowly begin to ebb. He had held an idea of Daisy so colossal, so far beyond anything reality could supply, that the living woman — charming, tearful, holding his shirts — could not quite equal the phantom he had nourished. There must have been moments even that afternoon when Daisy tumbled short of his dreams — not through her own fault, but because of the colossal vitality of his illusion. It had gone beyond her, beyond everything. He had thrown himself into it with a creative passion, adding to it all the time, decking it out with every bright feather that drifted his way. No amount of fire or freshness can challenge what a man will store up in his ghostly heart.

When Daisy left that evening, she left transformed. She drove away through the rain singing a little under her breath, and I have no doubt that for her the afternoon had been a resurrection: the world had handed back to her, intact, the worship of her eighteenth year, and added to it a mansion, a fortune, and a man who had crossed five years and an ocean of money to lay it all at her feet. Gatsby walked back to his house beside me in silence, and at the gate he turned and looked at my cottage as if it were a shrine. "She's there now," he said, almost to himself, meaning that she had stood in his rooms, touched his things, wept over his shirts — that the house was finally inhabited by the presence it had been built for. I asked him whether it had been worth the wait. He looked at me with an expression I could not read. "It wasn't a wait," he said. "It was a — preparation." Then he went in, and the lights came on one by one, floor by floor, as they had on a hundred nights when no one he loved was watching.

CHAPTER 7
The Golden Afternoons

After the reunion, everything changed. Gatsby's great house went dark. The parties ceased as abruptly as they had begun; the Rolls Royce no longer ferried guests from the station; the orchestra fell silent. When I asked after the change, I learned that Gatsby had dismissed every servant in the place and replaced them with people sent by Wolfsheim, "who wouldn't gossip" — for Daisy had begun to come over in the afternoons, and her visits were not the kind that could survive servants' talk. Sometimes she came alone. Sometimes, I suspected, the afternoons stretched into evenings. The whole apparatus of Gatsby's public magnificence, which had existed only to attract her attention, was dismantled the moment it had served its purpose, like scaffolding around a finished building.

I was drawn into the orbit of those afternoons more than I wished. Once I went with them to the Buchanan house for dinner — Gatsby, Daisy, Tom, Jordan, and I — and the evening was a study in strain. Tom watched Gatsby the way a man watches a stranger at his own table, and his dislike hardened visibly when Gatsby, looking at Daisy, said, "Your wife doesn't love you. She's never loved you. She loves me." He said it quietly, almost gently, but Tom's face changed. "You must be crazy," he said, and the matter was dropped only because dinner was announced, but nothing was dropped at all. Daisy, caught between the two men, tried to laugh it away, and her voice sounded artificial even to herself.

Gatsby's dream, I was beginning to see, was not simply to possess Daisy. It was to erase the five years between them — to go back to Louisville, to the white girlhood house, and begin again as if Tom Buchanan, the wedding, the child, had never existed. He wanted Daisy to go to Tom and say, "I never loved you," and after she had said it, they would return to Louisville and be married from her house, just as if it were five years before. I told him he was asking too much. "I wouldn't ask too much of her," he ventured. "You can't repeat the past." "Can't repeat the past?" he cried incredulously. "Why of course you can!" He looked around him wildly, as if the past were lurking here in the shadow of his house, just out of reach of his hand. He was determined, I understood, to recover something — some idea of himself, perhaps — that had gone into loving Daisy at first.

Meanwhile Tom had not been idle. He had made inquiries in the city, and what he had learned — rumors of bootlegging, of drugstores in Chicago and New York selling grain alcohol over the counter — he kept to himself with the patience of a man preparing an ambush. He was not a fool; he understood that Gatsby's money smelled, and he intended, at the right moment, to make Daisy smell it too.

And so the summer ripened toward its crisis. The heat that year was relentless. By the end of August, the whole of Long Island seemed to shimmer in it, and tempers shortened in drawing rooms and kitchens alike. I turned thirty that August, and I remember thinking, driving home on the train, that I was five years too old to lie to myself and call it honor. I did not know, that night, how soon the heat would break, and what it would break over all of us.

There was one other thing I noticed in those weeks, and I record it because it explained much that came later. Daisy had a little girl, Pammy, three years old, a round-faced child with yellow hair, and I almost never saw her. She appeared once at dinner, brought in by her nurse like a dessert, exclaimed over, and removed. Daisy, watching her go, said something that chilled me without my understanding why: she spoke of the child as if the child were an afterthought, a decorative consequence of a marriage she had already ceased to believe in. And I understood that when Gatsby demanded that Daisy erase the past, he was demanding she erase that child too — not in body, but in meaning. It was the one part of his dream he never examined, and it may have been the part that made the dream impossible. A man can ask a woman to say she never loved her husband. He cannot ask her to unhappen her own life, and somewhere, even in the golden afternoons, Daisy must have known it. Her voice, when she spoke of going away with him, had begun to carry the faintest note of a woman negotiating with herself — and Gatsby, listening only to the music of it, heard nothing but agreement.

CHAPTER 8
The Hottest Day

The day it happened was the hottest of the summer, a day when the very air seemed to have been baked. I lunched at the Buchanans' house in East Egg, where Gatsby and Jordan were also guests, and from the moment I arrived the tension was palpable. Daisy, fanning herself, suggested that we all go into town. "Who is 'Tom'?" she asked Gatsby, in a tone meant to wound her husband — and Tom, goaded, turned on Gatsby at last. There was a quarrel about who would drive which car: Tom insisted that Gatsby take his coupe into town, while he would drive Gatsby's great yellow motorcar. "I'll be damned if I let you take my wife in that circus wagon," was the sense of it, though he dressed it in politeness. Daisy, white-faced, chose to ride with Gatsby in Tom's coupe, and the rest of us climbed into the yellow car, Tom driving with a grim, gathering fury.

At a garage in the valley of ashes — Wilson's garage — we stopped for gasoline. Wilson came out to serve us, and he looked sick, as if he had been ill for days. He had discovered, he told Tom in an undertone I was not meant to hear, that his wife had a lover, though he did not know who it was, and he intended to take her away out West within a day or two. Tom stared at him. The realization moved through him visibly: if Wilson took Myrtle away, Tom would lose his mistress at the very moment he was fighting to keep his wife. "I've got my wife locked up in the room upstairs," Wilson muttered, "and she won't be able to get out for a long while." From an upper window, as we drove away, Myrtle's face looked down at us — looked, I thought, with a particular intensity at Jordan, whom she had mistaken for Daisy, for the woman in the yellow car.

We took rooms at the Plaza Hotel to escape the heat, and there, in a stifling suite with bowls of ice and a bottle of whisky, the confrontation came at last. Tom opened it. "What kind of a row are you trying to cause in my house anyhow?" he demanded. Gatsby, standing very still, replied: "Your wife doesn't love you. She's never loved you. She loves me." Tom laughed contemptuously. "You must be crazy." Then Gatsby said the thing he had rehearsed a thousand times: "She never loved you, do you hear? She only married you because I was poor and she was tired of waiting for me. It was a terrible mistake, but in her heart she never loved anyone except me."

It might have held. But Tom, who had done his homework, began methodically to destroy him. He laid out what his man in the city had found: that Gatsby and Wolfsheim had bought up a string of drugstores in New York and Chicago and sold grain alcohol over the counter; that this was only the first step toward something bigger and darker; that Gatsby was a common bootlegger, a criminal, a man with blood somewhere behind him. "I found out what your 'drugstores' were," Tom said triumphantly. "He and this Wolfsheim bought up a lot of side-street drugstores here and in Chicago and sold grain alcohol over the counter. That's one of his little stunts. I picked him for a bootlegger the first time I saw him, and I wasn't far wrong." Daisy shrank visibly from Gatsby as the words accumulated. I saw her draw further and further into herself, and I saw Gatsby see it too.

In the end Tom, with a cruelty that had the ring of total victory, told Gatsby to take Daisy home. "You two start on home, Daisy," he said, pointing at Gatsby. "In Mr. Gatsby's car." She was to go with him as a final demonstration that he was beneath fear — that Tom had nothing to be afraid of from such a man. "Go on," Tom added. "He won't annoy you. I think he realizes that his presumptuous little flirtation is over." They left together in the coupe. We followed later in the yellow car, through the gathering dusk, toward the valley of ashes, where George Wilson stood in his garage with a wife locked upstairs and a heart full of poisoned certainty, waiting for the yellow car to come back down the road.

As we drove back through the thickening dusk in the yellow car, Tom was in a state of suppressed exultation. He had won, and he knew it, and the knowledge made him almost genial. "I suppose the latest thing is to sit back and let Mr. Nobody from Nowhere make love to your wife," he said, to no one in particular, and then, with the magnanimity of victory, he began to speak of the future as if nothing had happened — of a horse he meant to buy, of people he meant to see. Jordan sat silent beside the window, watching the ash heaps slide past. I thought of Daisy, ahead of us in the coupe with a man whose world had just been dismantled in a hotel room, and I wondered what either of them was saying. Nothing, I think. I think they drove in a silence so complete that the road itself seemed to be holding its breath, and that the first sound either of them heard, when it came, was the sound of a woman's body striking the car.

CHAPTER 9
The Accident

What happened on the road that night I pieced together afterward from a dozen accounts — the witnesses at the inquest, Michaelis, the young Greek who ran the coffee shop next to Wilson's garage, and Gatsby himself, who told me the truth of it in the dark outside the Buchanan house. It came about this way.

Myrtle Wilson had been locked in a room above the garage all afternoon, and she had fought her husband like a wild animal to get out. Toward evening, watching through the window, she saw the great yellow car slow down and stop for gasoline, and she saw — as she believed — Tom Buchanan get out of it, and a woman beside him. Her jealousy fixed upon that image. Hours later, when the yellow car came down the road again out of the dusk, returning from the city, she broke from Wilson's grip, ran down the stairs, and rushed out into the road, waving her arms, certain that the man she loved was coming back for her at last.

The car did not stop. It swerved, and it struck her, and it went on into the night, dragging her body a little way before it broke free and left her lying in the dust of the road, her left breast swinging loose like a flap, her mouth wide open, the blood mixing with the gray ash of the valley. She was dead before Wilson reached her. He came out of the garage at the sound and stood over the body making a small, animal noise, and the crowd that gathered — truck drivers, passersby, Michaelis from the coffee shop — closed around him in the yellow light of the garage, murmuring. A witness swore the car was yellow, a big foreign car, and that it had not even hesitated.

The driver of that car was Daisy Buchanan. Gatsby told me so himself, in whispers, standing in the shadow of the Buchanans' lawn later that night, where he had stationed himself to watch the house in case Tom should hurt her. "Was Daisy driving?" I asked him. "Yes," he said, after a moment, "but of course I'll say I was. You see, when we left New York she was very nervous and she thought driving would steady her. And when we came to that garage, the girl came out and rushed at us — Daisy turned the wheel toward the other car, and then she lost her nerve and turned back. I tried to grab the wheel." He had taken the blame as instinctively as he had once reached for the green light. He intended, if the matter came to an arrest, to stand between Daisy and the law with his own body.

I went up to the Buchanan house and peered through a blind into the kitchen. Tom and Daisy sat opposite each other at the table, with a plate of cold fried chicken between them and two bottles of ale. He was talking earnestly, and she nodded now and then, and there was an unmistakable air of natural intimacy about the picture, as of two people who had closed ranks against the world. Whatever had happened at the Plaza, whatever had happened on the road, they were together in it now, conspiring. I left Gatsby standing in the moonlight, watching a house that no longer contained anything for him, and I told him to go home. He would not go. He wanted to wait, he said, just in case she needed him. "I want to wait here till Daisy goes to bed," he said. "Good night, old sport." I walked home through the dark, and I did not sleep well.

The inquest, when it came, settled nothing. A tired coroner, a handful of witnesses, a verdict of death by a hit-and-run driver unknown — the machinery of justice grinding over the facts and producing, as it so often does, a paperwork version of events that satisfied everyone except the people who knew the truth. Michaelis testified that Wilson had been half out of his mind with grief and suspicion. No one asked whose car it truly was. No one asked who had been at the wheel. Tom Buchanan's name was not mentioned, nor Daisy's, nor Gatsby's, except in the newspapers' listing of the car's owner — and even that was vague, a "prominent West Egg resident," unnamed. I read the accounts twice, and each time I was struck by how completely the real story had already been buried: a woman dead in the dust, a wife driving, a lover taking the blame, a husband locking the kitchen door of his conscience against the night — and the official record saw none of it. It saw a road, a car, an accident. Perhaps that is all that records are ever able to see.

CHAPTER 10
The Vigil

I could not leave him standing there alone all night, and yet there was nothing I could do for him. I lay awake in my cottage listening to the sounds of the summer night thinning toward dawn, and when I rose at last and went to his house, I found he had only just come in. We sat in his great hall while the light strengthened, and he told me, in fragments, the story he had never told anyone — the true one, this time, without the medals and the photographs.

He had been born James Gatz, the son of poor farmers in North Dakota, and at seventeen he had rowed out one morning to a yacht anchored in Lake Superior and warned its owner, a copper magnate named Dan Cody, of a storm that would have wrecked the boat. Cody took the boy aboard, and for five years Gatz sailed with him — steward, mate, skipper, secretary — learning the manners of wealth from the inside while Cody drank himself through a fortune and toward death. When Cody died, his mistress cheated the young man out of the twenty-five thousand dollars Cody had left him, and Gatz — who had already invented, in the white heat of his ambition, a new person named Jay Gatsby — set out to become the man he had imagined. It was in Louisville, in an officer's uniform that erased the shame of his poverty, that he had met Daisy Fay, and loved her with a concentration that had the force of a religion. He knew she was beyond him. He took her anyway, under false pretenses of wealth and family, and when the war took him overseas he clung to the memory of her as the one fixed star in his universe. He came home to find her married. Everything since — the drugstores, Wolfsheim, the mansion, the parties — had been the building of a ladder toward a single window.

"I can't describe to you how surprised I was to find out I loved her, old sport," he said, and there was no artifice in it now. He spoke of the first kiss, of how her lips had blossomed for him like a flower, and the incarnation was complete. He had wed his unutterable visions to her perishable breath, and his mind would never romp again like the mind of God.

That morning, Wilson came to my cottage asking questions. I did not know then how much he knew, or who had told him. He had been up all night with Michaelis, talking wildly, half-mad with grief, and toward morning he had begun to speak of the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg staring down at the valley. "God sees everything," he had repeated, again and again, and Michaelis, watching him, had been unable to tell whether he was comforted or condemned by the idea. Somewhere in that night of madness, Wilson had become certain that the owner of the yellow car was his wife's lover, and that the lover had killed her deliberately. Someone — and I have always believed it was Tom Buchanan, defending his own house with another man's life — had given him Gatsby's name and told him where Gatsby lived.

I warned Gatsby, when I left him that morning, that he should go away for a week, that the car could be traced, that he was in danger. He shook his head. He could not leave until he knew what Daisy was going to do. He meant, I think, to take the blame upon himself entirely — and he meant also, with the last stubbornness of his dream, to wait for a sign from her: a telephone call, a message, anything. The telephone, all that day, did not ring.

At noon I telephoned the Buchanan house myself, on some pretext I no longer remember, and the butler informed me in a voice of trained blankness that Mr. and Mrs. Buchanan were out, and could not say when they would return. I hung up with a coldness spreading through me that I tried, and failed, to argue away. Perhaps she was protecting herself. Perhaps she was protecting the child. Perhaps — and this was the thought I could not put down — the scene I had witnessed through the kitchen blind had been the truest one of the summer: two people at a table with a plate of cold chicken, bound not by love but by a shared instinct for survival, closing ranks against the consequences of their own carelessness. If that was so, then Gatsby was standing guard over an empty house, and the call he waited for had been decided against before the body in the road was cold. I worked late at the office to avoid going home. When the telephone on my desk rang at four, I started violently — but it was only Jordan, wanting to know whether I had heard anything, and telling me, with her cool golfer's fatalism, that Daisy and Tom had gone away. She did not know where. Nobody knew where. It was as if they had never existed, except in the damage.

CHAPTER 11
The End of the Dream

Gatsby had not used the swimming pool all summer, and on that last afternoon he decided, on an impulse, to take one swim before it was drained for the season. He had the chauffeur bring out an inflatable mattress, and he changed into his bathing suit and went down through the garden, past the rows of empty chairs where a hundred guests had once drunk his champagne, to the blue water. The leaves were beginning to turn. There was an autumn flavor in the air, a sense of endings, and the gardener had warned him that the pool would be emptied by evening.

Wilson came on foot, walking the miles from the valley of ashes with a revolver in his pocket and the name of his enemy like a coal in his mind. He had stopped at garages along the way, asking for the yellow car, asking for the man Gatsby, and the trail had led him, as trails of that kind always do, exactly where hatred needed it to lead. He came up through the garden without hurry. No one stopped him. The servants Wolfsheim had supplied were people who knew better than to see anything.

I was at my office in the city when it happened, and I learned of it in pieces, each piece worse than the last. They found Gatsby floating on the mattress in the pool, with a thin red circle of blood widening in the water around him, drifting a little with the slight current of the drainage. The chauffeur heard the shots — he thought they were firecrackers, at first — and by the time the household understood what had occurred, Wilson's body had been found a little way off in the grass, his own hand the instrument of his second death. The holocaust was complete. That was the phrase the newspapers would have used, if the newspapers had cared; as it was, they cared very little, and the reports were brief and garbled, and the name of the man who had thrown the greatest parties on Long Island was misspelled in two of them.

I went back to West Egg that evening on a train full of commuters reading about it over one another's shoulders, and I took charge of the house because there was no one else to take charge of it. The great rooms were empty and echoing. The telephone rang constantly with people who wanted details, not mourning — party guests, journalists, a girl from Chicago who wept and said her name was Catherine something, or other, and then hung up. Wolfsheim, when I reached him at last, refused to come. "I can't do it," he wrote. "I can't get mixed up in it. When a man gets killed I never like to get mixed up in it in any way. I keep out." Daisy and Tom had left East Egg that afternoon, with suitcases, no one knew where. She had not called. She had not sent a message. She had not even sent flowers.

I sat in Gatsby's hall that night among the crates and the dust sheets, and I thought of the green light, and of the arms stretched out across the water, and of how close the dream had been — close enough to touch, close enough to hold for one golden hour in my little cottage — and how it had dissolved, not because it was unworthy, but because the world it was aimed at was made of people who retreat into their money and their vast carelessness, and let others pay the bill. Gatsby had paid it. He had paid it for Daisy, and for Tom, and in a sense for all of them, and he had paid it believing to the end that the telephone would ring.

The police came and went with their notebooks and their incurious eyes. A detective photographed the pool, asked the chauffeur three questions, and left a card I threw away. The story they settled on was simple and satisfying to everyone concerned: a crazed garage keeper, a murdered millionaire of mysterious origins, a suicide. It had the shape of a newspaper morality tale — the bootlegger got what bootleggers get — and it absolved, by its very simplicity, everyone who might otherwise have had to answer for a woman dead in the ashes and a man dead in his own blue water. I was interviewed once, briefly, and I said almost nothing, and I have wondered since whether my silence was loyalty or cowardice. At the time it felt like both. Gatsby had spent his whole fortune of selfhood protecting Daisy's name; it seemed a poor return to spend it for him in a police station. So the truth of that summer went where such truths usually go — into the keeping of the one person who could do nothing with it but remember.

CHAPTER 12
The Funeral and the Shore

I wanted no one else to arrange the funeral. It seemed to me that I was the only friend he had, and I set about the work with a furious loyalty, telephoning everyone whose name I had ever heard in connection with his — the party guests, the business associates, Wolfsheim, the hundreds of people who had eaten his food and drunk his wine all summer long. Almost no one came. Wolfsheim sent a letter of magnificent evasion. The owl-eyed man from the library, whose name turned out to be Klipspringer — no, Klipspringer was the boarder who lived in the house and would not even come downstairs for it, pleading a prior engagement in a voice I can still hear. In the end, the man who did come was the one from the library, the little stout fellow with the enormous spectacles, who had marveled at the real books. "They used to go there by the hundreds," he said, standing with me in the rain beside the grave. "I can't believe it. I can't believe he's dead." He removed his spectacles and wiped them, inside and out, and his eyes were red. "The poor son-of-a-bitch," he said.

One other person came: an old man in a worn suit, who arrived on the train from Minnesota and stood bewildered in the great empty house, looking at the staircase and the marble and the portraits of men his son had never known. It was Gatsby's father, Henry C. Gatz, and he was proud — proudly, innocently proud — of everything his boy had built. He showed me a photograph of the house, worn soft at the edges, that Jimmy had sent him, and a battered copy of a boyhood book in which the young James Gatz had written out a daily schedule of self-improvement: rising hours, dumbbell exercises, study, work, and a list of general resolves — no more smoking or chewing, be better to parents, read one improving book a week. "Jimmy was bound to get ahead," the old man said. "He always had some resolves like that." If he had lived, he said, he'd have been a great man. A man like James J. Hill. He'd have helped build up the country. I did not tell him how his son had built what he built, or what it had cost, or for whom. It would have served no purpose. The dead do not need our honesty, and the old man needed his pride.

On the morning of the funeral, I found that some boy, wandering in from the road, had scrawled an obscene word in chalk on the white steps of the house — the summer's whole judgment of Gatsby, delivered by a child with a piece of chalk. I found a cloth and a bucket and I erased it myself, on my knees, before the hearse came. It was the last service I could do him, and I did it with a thoroughness that surprised me, as if the cleanliness of those steps were somehow the whole of the case I meant to argue to the world: that he had been better than his reputation, finer than his fortune, and worthier than the people for whom he died. Then I went in and dressed, and waited for the rain, and for the three cars that were all the procession the king of West Egg was going to get.

After the funeral I could not stay in the East. The whole region had become haunted for me — that huge, incoherent failure of a house across the lawn, the white and gold of East Egg across the water, the ash heaps where Myrtle Wilson had died reaching for a life that was never hers. I settled my affairs, sold my car, and took the train west. On my last night in West Egg I walked down to the beach and lay on the sand and looked across at the darkened shore, where the green light at the end of Daisy's dock would have been, had anyone been there to switch it on. I thought of Gatsby's wonder when he first picked out that light, and of how he had believed, with the whole force of a self-invented soul, that the past could be recovered and lived again, better.

And I thought, as the moon rose over the cooling water, of all the others who had stood on shores like this one and looked out at new worlds with the same astonished hope — the first Dutch sailors who saw this continent fresh and green before them, and felt, for a transitory enchanted moment, that man's capacity for wonder had found its matching object at last. Gatsby's dream was only the last and grandest version of that oldest American dream: that somewhere, just across the water, the future is waiting, and it will be finer than anything we have yet imagined. Tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther — and one fine morning, so we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.
