The Power of Habit
Charles Duhigg

CHAPTER 1
The Man Who Could Not Remember

In the autumn of 1993, a man named Eugene Pauly walked into a laboratory in San Diego and, without knowing it, changed what scientists understood about the human mind. Eugene was in his seventies. A few years earlier, a viral infection had attacked his brain and destroyed a small structure near its centre called the medial temporal lobe. The damage was precise and devastating. Eugene could remember almost nothing new. He would meet his doctors, shake their hands, chat pleasantly, and then, minutes later, greet them again as complete strangers.

His wife, Beverly, cared for him at home and learned to live inside his narrow window of time. Eugene could hold a conversation. He could discuss events from decades earlier in vivid detail. But ask him what he had eaten for breakfast, or whether he had taken his morning walk, and he had no idea. Doctors who tested him found that his intelligence, his language and his personality were all intact. Only his memory of the present had been erased, as if each moment slid off him like water off glass.

And yet Eugene's life ran with a strange smoothness. Every morning he rose, made himself breakfast — sometimes twice, forgetting he had already eaten — and took a walk around the neighbourhood. He always returned home, even though he could not have drawn a map of his own street or named the houses he passed. When researchers followed him, they saw that he navigated by landmarks he did not know he knew. He turned at the right corners. He avoided the wrong streets. Somewhere inside his damaged brain, a system older and deeper than memory was guiding his feet.

One day Beverly tried an experiment the doctors had suggested. She asked Eugene to draw a map of their block. He could not. She asked him to point to his own house from the front garden. He hesitated. But when he needed the bathroom in the middle of the night, he found it without error. When he was hungry, he walked to the kitchen, opened the correct cupboard, and took out a jar of nuts he did not remember buying. His body knew things his mind had never been told.

Eugene became one of the most studied patients in neuroscience, and what he taught science was this: habits do not live in the same place as memory. Deep in the brain sits a structure called the basal ganglia, a cluster of tissue that evolved long before conscious thought. It stores the patterns of our repeated actions — the sequences we have practised so often that they no longer require thinking. Eugene's basal ganglia were untouched by his illness. His memories were gone, but his habits remained, and they were strong enough to run much of his life.

This discovery matters far beyond one man's tragedy. It suggests that every one of us carries two systems at once: a thinking self that plans, decides and remembers, and an older, quieter self that simply repeats what has worked before. When you drive home along a familiar route and arrive with no memory of the journey, the second self was driving. When you find a biscuit in your hand before you have decided to eat one, the second self reached first. Eugene's condition was extreme, but the machinery inside him is the same machinery inside you, operating every day.

Scientists who studied Eugene ran an experiment that revealed how this machinery works. They placed him in front of a computer screen showing pairs of shapes and asked him to guess, for each pair, which shape hid a reward. Healthy people learn the pattern within a few dozen tries because they remember their past guesses. Eugene never learned it consciously — every round felt brand new to him. Yet over weeks, his accuracy quietly climbed to near perfection. He was learning without knowing he was learning. His basal ganglia were recording the pattern even though his conscious mind recorded nothing.

When the researchers asked him why he chose one shape over another, Eugene could only shrug. "I just felt it was right," he said. That feeling — the pull toward a familiar action, operating beneath awareness — is the force this book is about. Habits are not destiny, but they are powerful, and they follow rules. Once you understand the rules, you can work with them instead of against them. Eugene could not remember his own address, yet his habits carried him home. The question for the rest of us is whether our habits are carrying us where we actually want to go.

Beverly kept notes on all of this, the way a scientist's spouse becomes a scientist by necessity. She recorded which routes Eugene took, what he reached for in the kitchen, how he spent the identical hours of his identical days. What her notes showed was a man whose life, stripped of memory, had become pure pattern — and the pattern was coherent, even graceful. Eugene was not stumbling through his days. He was being carried by a structure that remembered for him. The tragedy of his condition is obvious. The lesson of it is subtler: the structure that carried Eugene is not a backup system that switches on only when memory fails. In healthy brains it runs constantly, underneath everything, and most of us simply never notice it because our memory takes the credit.

CHAPTER 2
The Habit Loop

To understand how habits work, it helps to watch a brain learn one from the very beginning. At the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, researchers implanted tiny sensors in the brains of rats and placed the animals in a simple maze. At one end stood a partition. When the rat heard a click, the partition opened, and the rat could wander the maze to find a piece of chocolate waiting at the far end. The scientists watched the animals' brains as they learned, and what they saw became the foundation of modern habit science.

The first time a rat ran the maze, its brain was busy the whole way through. It sniffed, scratched, peered around corners, and its mental activity stayed high from start to finish — the brain was working hard, taking in everything. But after dozens of runs, something changed. The rat stopped sniffing and dithering. It shot through the maze in a clean, practised line. And its brain activity collapsed. During the run itself, the brain went almost quiet, as if it had handed the job to an autopilot. Spikes of activity remained only at the very beginning, when the rat heard the click, and at the very end, when it reached the chocolate.

The researchers realised they were watching a process called chunking. The brain converts a sequence of actions — hear click, run, turn left, find chocolate — into a single packaged routine that can be launched as one unit. Once a behaviour is chunked, the thinking parts of the brain can rest. This is an enormous evolutionary advantage. A brain that had to consciously deliberate over every step of every routine would be slow, exhausted and quickly eaten. Chunking lets us tie our shoes, brew coffee and reverse a car out of a driveway while our minds wander elsewhere.

From the rat experiments, scientists drew a simple diagram that explains nearly every habit ever studied. They called it the habit loop, and it has three parts. First comes the cue: a trigger that tells your brain to switch into automatic mode and which routine to use. The click of the partition was a cue. So is the sight of your phone, the smell of a bakery, a particular time of day, a feeling of boredom or stress. Second comes the routine: the behaviour itself, which can be physical, mental or emotional — running the maze, pouring a drink, snapping at a colleague. Third comes the reward: something that helps your brain decide whether this loop is worth remembering. The chocolate taught the rat's brain that the maze was worth chunking.

Over time, this loop — cue, routine, reward — becomes more and more automatic. The cue and the reward grow neurologically intertwined until the cue alone produces a sense of anticipation, even a craving, for the reward. That is when a habit is truly born: not when you perform the behaviour, but when the trigger by itself makes you want it. The commuter who passes a particular café begins to taste the coffee before she has decided to stop. The phone buzzes, and your hand is moving before your mind has voted.

The loop explains why habits are so hard to break through willpower alone. A habit is not stored as a decision you can revisit; it is stored as a reflex wired into some of the oldest tissue in the brain. You cannot simply delete it, any more than the rat could unlearn the maze. But the loop also contains good news. Because a habit is a structure with parts, it can be studied part by part. Find the cue, and you can predict the behaviour. Understand the reward, and you can understand what the behaviour is really for. The loop is a machine, and machines can be redesigned.

It is worth noticing how universal this machinery is. The same loop drives the smoker's break, the athlete's warm-up, the writer's morning ritual and the toddler's bedtime routine. Organisations run on loops too, and so do markets and whole societies. Nearly half of what you did today was probably not decided today at all; studies suggest that roughly forty percent of daily actions are performed in nearly the same situations each day, driven by cues rather than choices. We like to believe we are the authors of our days. Much of the time, we are the editors of patterns written long ago.

That is not a counsel of despair. It is a map. In the chapters ahead, we will meet the scientists, executives, coaches and communities who learned to read the map — and discovered that the loop, once seen clearly, becomes a lever. A habit cannot be eradicated, but it can be reshaped, redirected and rebuilt. Everything begins with learning to see the loop operating in your own life: the cue you did not notice, the routine you did not choose, the reward you never named.

CHAPTER 3
The Craving Brain

At the beginning of the twentieth century, an advertising executive named Claude Hopkins was approached with an unusual business proposition. A friend wanted him to promote a new product: a minty toothpaste called Pepsodent. Hopkins was sceptical. Almost nobody in America brushed their teeth at the time. The nation's dental health was so poor that it had become a matter of military concern, yet people simply did not see the point of toothpaste. Selling it looked like a losing battle.

Hopkins agreed to try, and the campaign he built became one of the most famous in advertising history. Instead of lecturing the public about decay, his advertisements invited readers to run their tongues across their teeth. You will feel a film, the ads said — a dingy coating that makes your teeth look dull and invites decay. Pepsodent, the ads promised, removed that film and left teeth beautiful and white. Sales exploded. Within a few years Pepsodent was sold around the world, and within three decades, brushing had become a daily ritual for the majority of Americans.

Hopkins believed his success came from two rules he had discovered. First, find a simple, obvious trigger. Second, clearly define the reward. The film on your teeth was the trigger — a cue anyone could detect at any moment. Sparkling white teeth were the reward. Hopkins wrote proudly about this formula in his memoirs, and advertisers treated it as gospel for generations.

But there was a missing ingredient Hopkins never understood, and it explains why Pepsodent succeeded where dozens of rival toothpastes with identical claims failed. Pepsodent's formula included citric acid and mint oils — ingredients that irritated the mouth slightly and produced a cool, tingling sensation. That tingle did nothing for dental health. What it did was teach users to crave it. If people forgot to brush, they missed the tingle; their mouths felt strangely incomplete, not quite clean. The tingle became proof, at a sensory level, that the product was working. Other toothpastes cleaned teeth just as well, but they left the mouth feeling exactly as it had before, and their users drifted away.

This is the third element of habit formation, the one that turns a simple loop into a self-sustaining engine: craving. Neuroscientists demonstrated its power in experiments with monkeys trained to expect juice when a shape appeared on a screen. At first the monkeys' brains spiked with pleasure when the juice arrived. But as the habit formed, the spike moved earlier and earlier, until the brains lit up at the mere sight of the shape — before any juice appeared. The animals had begun to crave. When researchers then withheld the juice, the monkeys grew agitated, distracted and miserable. The cue had become a promise the brain demanded be kept.

Craving is what makes the habit loop spin on its own. A cue that merely triggers a routine produces a behaviour you perform when reminded. A cue that triggers a craving produces a behaviour you pursue. This is why the jogger feels restless on rest days, why the smoker's hand drifts toward a pocket, why a phone's notification badge can hijack an entire afternoon. The brain has learned to anticipate the reward, and anticipation is a far stronger fuel than the reward itself.

Understanding craving changes how you build good habits. If you want exercise to stick, the lesson of Pepsodent says the workout needs a reward your brain can learn to anticipate — and the reward must be vivid and immediate, not a vague promise of health in thirty years. People who exercise consistently, studies find, usually crave something concrete: the endorphin lift afterwards, the satisfaction of recording a completed session, the smoothie or shower that follows. Over time, the craving attaches to the cue — the sight of running shoes by the door — and the behaviour begins to feel less like discipline and more like appetite.

Craving also explains why breaking bad habits is so uncomfortable. When you deny a loop its reward, you are not merely skipping a behaviour; you are disappointing a brain that has already begun celebrating. The itch, the irritability, the sense that something is missing — that is craving, and it fades only when the brain learns a new expectation. You cannot simply starve a craving forever. But, as we will see next, you can give it a new routine to feed on. Hopkins sold toothpaste by manufacturing a craving nobody knew they had. The same force, understood honestly, can be turned toward habits that serve us.

Once you see craving clearly, you start seeing it everywhere it has been engineered. The slot machine's near-miss, the social feed that never quite ends, the streak counter that punishes a missed day, the mint tingle in the toothpaste — all are attempts to move the brain's pleasure spike from the reward to the cue, so that anticipation does the work of selling. This is not a reason for paranoia; it is a reason for literacy. A craving you can name is a craving you can negotiate with. A craving that operates unnamed simply feels like your own desire, and you will obey it while believing you chose it. Much of the rest of this book is about what happens when people — coaches, executives, doctors, pastors — learn to work with this force deliberately instead of being worked by it.

CHAPTER 4
The Golden Rule of Habit Change

In 1996, the Tampa Bay Buccaneers were one of the worst teams in American football. They had not posted a winning season in over a decade, and their locker room had the resigned air of men waiting to lose. Then the team hired a new head coach, Tony Dungy, who arrived with a philosophy that sounded almost insultingly simple. He would not rebuild the team's playbook. He would not demand that his players think more on the field. Instead, he would change their habits — by changing as little as possible.

Dungy's insight came from studying how players actually behave under pressure. In the chaos of a game, athletes do not rise to the level of their intentions; they fall to the level of their habits. A defender who has to consciously analyse a play will always be a half-second too slow. So Dungy drilled his players on a small number of cues — the position of an opponent's feet, the angle of a shoulder, the formation across the line — and attached one automatic response to each. Read the cue, execute the routine, no deliberation. He kept the triggers the players already saw and the rewards they already wanted, and he swapped in new routines between them.

It took years, and there were heartbreaking losses along the way, including the death of Dungy's public reputation for being too calm to win. But the habits took hold. Tampa Bay became one of the league's most feared defences. Dungy later moved to Indianapolis, applied the same method, and won the Super Bowl. His approach worked because it respected a principle that researchers now call the Golden Rule of habit change: you cannot extinguish a bad habit. You can only change it — and to change it, you must keep the old cue and deliver the old reward, but insert a new routine.

The science behind the rule is sobering. Studies of people who have successfully quit smoking, overeating or other destructive patterns almost never find that the old habit vanished. Brain scans show the old loop still present, sometimes decades later, waiting for its cue. What changed is that a new routine grew alongside it, strong enough to win the contest when the cue fires. The ex-smoker who chews gum, walks around the block or calls a friend at the moment of craving has not deleted the smoking loop. He has built a rival loop on the same foundations.

Alcoholics Anonymous, the most successful habit-change organisation in history, is built on exactly this architecture, whether or not its founders knew the neuroscience. AA does not ask alcoholics to stop wanting relief, belonging or escape — the rewards drinking provided. It keeps the cues, too: the stress, the loneliness, the end of the working day. What it supplies is a new routine to perform when those cues arrive: go to a meeting, call a sponsor, speak honestly to a room of people who understand. The rewards — relief, connection, calm — arrive through a different door.

Researchers who study habit change add one more ingredient that AA understood intuitively: belief. In experiments, people who changed habits successfully often differed from those who failed not in the routines they used but in whether they believed change was possible — and belief frequently grew inside a group. Being surrounded by others who had done it made the new routine credible on the days when craving argued otherwise. Dungy's players believed because they saw the system work on the field. The alcoholic believes because the room is full of evidence. The loop provides the mechanism; belief provides the fuel to keep using it until the new routine becomes automatic.

The Golden Rule gives anyone a practical template. Whatever habit you want to change, start by respecting its structure. Do not wage war on the cue — cues are woven into your environment, your schedule and your emotions, and most cannot be removed. Do not sneer at the reward — it is a real need asking to be met. Instead, ask the engineer's question: when this cue fires, what new routine could deliver a similar reward? Then practise that routine the way Dungy's players practised their reads: deliberately, repeatedly, in low-stakes moments, until the day pressure arrives and the new habit is the one that shows up.

The rule also explains a puzzle that frustrates many people: why insight alone changes so little. A smoker can know everything about lungs and still smoke, because knowledge speaks to the conscious mind while the loop runs elsewhere. Knowing your cue and reward is necessary — the final chapter turns that knowledge into a step-by-step method — but the change itself happens at the level of the routine, practised until the new path is worn as smooth as the old one. Therapists who treat habits most successfully tend to work exactly this way. They spend little time forbidding the old behaviour and a great deal of time rehearsing its replacement: what you will do at the party, at the desk, at the moment the phone lights up. Willpower shouts at the routine. The Golden Rule quietly replaces it.

There is a corollary worth holding onto. Because the old loop is never erased, environments matter enormously. The recovered alcoholic who avoids the old bar is not being weak; he is being accurate about his own neurology. He knows the cue will fire the craving, and that his new routine, however strong, wins more easily when it is not ambushed hourly. Changing a habit, in other words, is partly an inside job and partly an act of stage design — arranging your surroundings so the new routine is the path of least resistance and the old one requires effort to reach. We will return to that idea when we look at how organisations and even whole companies arrange the stage for millions of people at once.

CHAPTER 5
Keystone Habits

In 1987, a man named Paul O'Neill stood before a room of Wall Street investors to introduce himself as the new chief executive of Alcoa, a giant aluminium manufacturer that was widely considered sluggish and troubled. The investors expected talk of profits, costs and restructuring. Instead, O'Neill told them he intended to focus on one thing: worker safety. He announced a goal of zero injuries — not fewer injuries, zero — in a heavy industry where molten metal, crushing machinery and serious accidents were treated as facts of life. The room was bewildered. One investor later said he hurried to a payphone and told his clients to sell.

That investor made a costly mistake. Within a year of O'Neill's arrival, Alcoa's profits hit record levels. By the time he retired, the company's net income was roughly five times larger than when he started, its market value had grown by tens of billions of dollars, and its injury rate had fallen to a small fraction of the national average. O'Neill had not begun with a financial strategy. He had begun with a habit — and the habit had reorganised the entire company.

Safety was what scientists call a keystone habit: a single pattern that, once changed, sets off a chain reaction that reshapes everything around it. O'Neill understood the mechanism instinctively. To drive injuries toward zero, Alcoa had to change how it communicated. Any worker could report a hazard, and union officials gained a direct line to the president. Managers had to investigate every injury within twenty-four hours and produce a plan to prevent its return. Those requirements forced new routines everywhere: faster information flow, honest reporting instead of blame, maintenance done before machines failed rather than after. A company that learned to talk truthfully about safety found it could talk truthfully about quality, cost and everything else. One habit had levered open the whole culture.

Keystone habits work because they create small, visible wins and because they change identity, not just behaviour. When a factory floor goes a month without an injury, workers begin to see themselves as people who run a careful operation, and that self-image quietly disciplines a hundred other decisions. Psychologists find the same pattern in individuals. People who begin exercising regularly — even modestly — often start eating better, working more productively, smoking less and feeling more patient, without consciously targeting any of those things. The exercise habit seems to spill over, reorganising life around it.

The phenomenon has a structure. Keystone habits matter more than most habits because they create cultures where change becomes contagious, and they give people a sense of agency that grows with each success. A dieter who starts keeping a simple food journal — writing down everything eaten — often loses twice as much weight as dieters who do not, researchers found, not because the journal burns calories but because the act of recording creates awareness, planning and a daily moment of self-confrontation. The journal is a keystone: small, concrete, and quietly transformative of everything downstream.

For anyone trying to change, the practical lesson is to stop trying to change everything at once. Sweeping resolutions fail because they declare war on dozens of loops simultaneously, and the brain defends them all. Instead, find the keystone. Ask which single habit, if established, would make other good habits easier and other bad habits harder. For one person it is a morning walk that stabilises sleep, mood and appetite. For another it is making the bed, planning the day, or sitting down to one family meal. For a company it might be safety, or honest reporting, or the discipline of a weekly review.

O'Neill's story also contains a warning. He succeeded partly because safety was a value nobody inside Alcoa could publicly oppose — not the unions, not the managers, not the board. A keystone habit works best when it aligns people who normally disagree, giving them a shared standard that justifies uncomfortable changes. Choose a keystone that requires fighting everyone, and the chain reaction may never start. Choose one that everyone can stand behind, and resistance dissolves into a common project. One habit, chosen well, can move an institution of sixty thousand people. Chosen well in a single life, it can do the same.

Families have keystones too. Researchers who study households find that one of the most powerful is the regular family dinner. Children in families that eat together most evenings tend, on average, to earn better grades, show greater emotional control and report more confidence — not because of anything magical in the meal itself, but because the dinner is a delivery system: for conversation, for parental attention, for the daily rehearsal of manners, plans and values. Families rarely transform themselves by resolving to communicate better in general. They change by protecting one recurring hour in which communication is the unavoidable routine.

The search for a keystone is therefore a strategic question disguised as a personal one. It asks: where is the leverage in this system? Which loop, if I rebuild it, rebuilds others for free? The answer is rarely the habit that shames you most. It is usually something smaller, earlier in the day or higher in the chain of causes — sleep before productivity, planning before spending, the walk before the mood that makes the evening go well. Find the first domino, and you may not need to push the rest.

CHAPTER 6
Starbucks and the Willpower Muscle

Willpower is often described as a trait — something you either have or lack, like height or perfect pitch. A famous series of experiments suggests it is something else entirely: a skill that behaves like a muscle. In one study, students were led into a room filled with the smell of fresh-baked cookies. On the table sat a plate of the cookies and a bowl of radishes. Half the students were told to eat the cookies; the other half were told to eat only radishes and resist the cookies entirely. Afterwards, both groups were given a puzzle to work on — a puzzle secretly designed to be unsolvable. The cookie-eaters persisted for a long time. The radish-eaters, who had spent their willpower resisting temptation, gave up far sooner, visibly frustrated. Their self-control had been used up like a battery.

This finding — that willpower is a finite resource that depletes with use and strengthens with exercise — has been replicated in many forms. People who resist dessert at lunch are more likely to snap at colleagues in the afternoon. Students who force themselves to sit still and focus make worse decisions later. And like a muscle, willpower responds to training: in one study, students assigned to practise small acts of self-discipline, such as improving their posture for two weeks, performed significantly better on unrelated tests of self-control afterwards. Their willpower had grown stronger across their whole lives.

Starbucks built an empire partly by understanding this. When the coffee chain was expanding explosively in the 1990s and 2000s, executives realised their central problem was not coffee but people: thousands of young employees had to remain patient and cheerful while facing tired, rushed and sometimes rude customers — precisely the moments when willpower fails. A barista who has been yelled at by three customers in an hour is a person whose self-control muscle is exhausted. Snapping back is not a character flaw; it is depletion.

So Starbucks turned willpower into a habit. The company wrote workbooks, used in training, that did something clever: they identified the cues that typically preceded an employee's loss of control — an angry customer, a sudden rush, a broken machine — and pre-scripted new routines for those exact moments. One famous framework taught staff, when they felt their patience cracking, to follow a set sequence: listen to the customer, acknowledge their complaint, take action to solve the problem, thank them, and explain why the experience fell short. Employees practised these routines in role-play until they were automatic. The company even left blank pages in the workbook where workers wrote out their own plans for their personal hardest moments — when this cue hits, I will do this routine.

The effect was to move self-control out of the fragile realm of in-the-moment decision-making and into the sturdy realm of habit. A barista facing a furious customer no longer had to improvise grace under fire; the cue triggered a rehearsed sequence, the way a defender's cue triggered Tony Dungy's routines. Starbucks executives credited this training with improving customer satisfaction and reducing staff turnover — and the company famously invested in it even during the financial crisis, when cutting training would have been easier.

The same approach works in ordinary life, and researchers have a name for the tool: implementation intentions, or if-then plans. A smoker who decides in advance — if it is my coffee break, then I will walk to the park bench with a colleague — has converted a future willpower battle into a pre-loaded habit. Students who write down exactly when and where they will study, anticipating their likely distractions, dramatically outperform students who merely resolve to study more. The plan does the deciding in advance, while the willpower muscle is fresh, so that in the depleted moment there is nothing left to decide.

There is a deeper point here about kindness toward ourselves. If willpower is a muscle, then a day of failures is often a day of exhaustion, not a verdict on character. People who understand this stop relying on heroic resistance at 9 p.m. and start designing their mornings: fewer temptations left lying around, routines scripted for predictable hard moments, small daily exercises that build the muscle over time. Starbucks spends millions teaching teenagers this lesson. The rest of us can learn it free: do not trust your tired self to improvise. Give your fresh self the job of writing the script.

Starbucks' most famous proof of the method was a young man named Travis, who grew up in a household wrecked by addiction and cycled through fast-food jobs he could not keep, undone by lateness and a temper he could not govern. At Starbucks he was taught, patiently and explicitly, the routines this chapter describes — including how to recognise the first heat of anger as a cue and run a rehearsed sequence instead of an explosion. Within a few years Travis was managing a store of his own, and later supervising more. Nothing about his past had been edited. What changed was that an institution had handed his willpower a set of habits to stand on, at exactly the moments when willpower alone had always collapsed.

Notice what the company did not do. It did not hire only naturally patient people, and it did not merely exhort everyone to try harder — the two strategies most organisations and most individuals default to. It assumed self-control would fail at predictable moments and engineered those moments in advance. That assumption, humble as it sounds, is the dividing line between hoping for change and designing for it.

CHAPTER 7
When Organisations Run on Habit

Companies like to describe themselves through org charts, mission statements and strategy decks. The truth is less tidy: organisations run on habits just as individuals do. Thousands of unwritten routines — how meetings actually start, whose phone call gets returned, what happens to bad news on its way up — form the real operating system of any institution. These patterns exist because, at some point, they offered a reward: they avoided conflict, saved effort, or kept the peace. And because no one officially chose them, they can be almost impossible to see from inside.

Organisational habits are not inherently bad. In fact, they often arise as truces. In any company, departments compete for resources and status, and open warfare would be paralysing. So routines evolve that let rivals coexist: budgets get divided by last year's proportions, information gets shared in careful rituals, blame gets routed along predictable paths. These truces keep the organisation functioning day to day. But a truce can also protect a dysfunction long past the point where anyone would defend it out loud — and sometimes the bill arrives all at once.

Consider a hospital in Rhode Island that was, by many measures, excellent: prestigious surgeons, advanced equipment, dedicated nurses. Yet the hospital developed a culture with a dangerous habit at its core. Surgeons were treated as untouchable. Nurses who spotted a surgeon's mistake were expected to hint rather than speak plainly, and those who challenged a doctor directly risked humiliation or worse. Everyone knew the routine. The cue was a surgeon making an error; the routine was silence, deference or coded language; the reward was a shift without confrontation. The truce held — until it didn't.

In one case, an elderly man was brought in for brain surgery, and the operation was performed on the wrong side of his head. In another, a tonsillectomy patient — a teenager — died after a series of miscommunications in which staff concerns failed to travel up the hierarchy with the force they deserved. Investigations found no single villain. They found something more unsettling: a whole institution whose habits made it structurally difficult for the right person to say the right words at the right moment. The wrong-site surgery was not caused by incompetence. It was caused by routines that everyone followed and nobody owned.

What happened next is instructive. The hospital was fined, shamed in the press, and placed under scrutiny. In the crisis, leadership finally gained something that had been impossible in peacetime: the authority to rewrite the truces. New routines were imposed and drilled — surgical checklists that required the whole team to pause and confirm the patient, the procedure and the site aloud; explicit permission, even obligation, for any staff member to halt an operation by voicing a concern; cameras in operating rooms to make the new habits stick. The same crisis that exposed the habits created the one moment when they could be changed, because in a crisis the old rewards — comfort, deference, peace — suddenly look worthless next to survival.

This pattern appears far beyond hospitals. Companies in crisis routinely discover a capacity for change that eluded them for decades: NASA after its shuttle disasters rebuilt its safety routines; manufacturers after scandals suddenly find they can reorganise production lines that were supposedly immovable. Wise leaders learn to use such moments rather than waste them. Some even learn to manufacture the sense of urgency — honestly, without faking disaster — by making the costs of current habits vivid and undeniable, so that the organisation grants itself permission to change.

For the rest of us, the lesson is diagnostic. When a team, a family or a company keeps producing the same bad outcome despite everyone's good intentions, the problem is rarely the people. It is the loop: a cue nobody notices, a routine everybody follows, a reward — usually peace, or the avoidance of an awkward conversation — that keeps the whole pattern fed. Change efforts that lecture individuals while leaving the loop intact will fail, politely and repeatedly. Change efforts that find the truce, name it, and rewrite the routine in a moment of genuine urgency can transform institutions that looked unchangeable. Organisations, like people, do not rise to their values. They fall to their habits.

The checklist deserves a closer look, because it shows what replacing a truce actually involves. The old routine — defer, hint, stay silent — was not attacked with posters about speaking up. It was displaced by a new routine so concrete that it could be performed under stress: before any incision, the team stops; names and roles are stated; the patient, the procedure and the site are confirmed aloud; concerns are invited by name. Aviation had learned the same lesson decades earlier, after crashes in which junior officers died knowing something was wrong, having hinted at it too politely to their captains. Crew resource management — the discipline that flattened cockpit hierarchy in emergencies — was medicine's template. In both industries, the fix was not braver individuals. It was a script that made the brave thing the automatic thing.

Results in hospitals that adopted such routines have been striking: studies of surgical safety checklists across multiple countries found complications and deaths falling by roughly a third. Yet the deeper point is about timing. The Rhode Island hospital had possessed the knowledge to fix its culture for years. What it lacked, until disgrace arrived, was the organisational permission — the shared sense that the old truces were no longer acceptable and that anyone, however senior, could be asked to change. Crisis supplied that permission at a terrible price. The art of leadership, in large part, is finding ways to grant that permission without waiting for the catastrophe.

CHAPTER 8
The Company That Knew You First

A statistician named Andrew Pole joined the retail giant Target and was given an assignment that sounded like science fiction: figure out, from shopping data alone, which customers were pregnant — ideally before they had told anyone else. The reason was commercial and cold-blooded. New parents are among the most valuable customers in retail, buying everything from nappies to furniture for years. But they are also bombarded by every competitor at the moment the baby arrives, when birth records become public. The fortune would go to whoever reached them first, during pregnancy, when habits were still up for grabs.

Pole started by studying the purchases of women who had signed up for Target's baby registry, comparing what they bought as their due dates approached. Patterns emerged with startling clarity. In the second trimester, many women suddenly bought large quantities of unscented lotion. Around the same period came supplements — calcium, magnesium, zinc. As the due date neared, purchases shifted to unscented soap, hand sanitiser, washcloths and cotton balls, the unmistakable signature of someone preparing a hygienic nursery. Pole combined about twenty-five such products into a model that could assign each shopper a pregnancy prediction score — and often estimate the due date closely enough to send coupons timed to each stage.

The system worked so well that it produced an episode that has become famous. A man arrived at a Target store near Minneapolis, furious, demanding to see the manager. His teenage daughter had received a mailer full of coupons for baby clothes and cribs. He accused the store of trying to encourage her. The manager apologised. A few days later, the father called back, subdued: he had spoken with his daughter, and it turned out she was indeed pregnant, due in August. Target's habits model had learned something about the household before the household's own family knew it.

Beneath the creepiness lies a profound insight into how habits actually change. Pole and his colleagues understood that a person's purchasing routines are among the most stable things about them — people buy the same cereal, the same soap, in the same store, for years. Trying to break those routines head-on almost always fails. But during major life events — marriage, a move, a new job, a baby — habits soften. The routines are disrupted anyway, cues are scrambled, and people become briefly, unusually open to new patterns. Marketers call these windows life's inflection points. The entire pregnancy project was a machine for finding people standing inside such a window.

Target also learned a second, subtler lesson about how people react to feeling known. When the company sent mailers that were obviously built from surveillance — baby coupons and nothing else — customers were unsettled. So Target began camouflaging: the baby coupons were mixed in with offers for lawnmowers and wine glasses, products the customer had never bought, so the mailer looked like a random assortment that happened to contain uncannily useful bargains. Sales soared. People will use a coupon that feels like luck. They recoil from one that feels like a dossier.

For the individual reader, this chapter is a mirror. Your habits are not private in the way you imagine; they leak through every receipt, click and routine, and sophisticated organisations are reading them to predict what you will do next — sometimes before you know yourself. But the mirror also shows the way out. The moments when companies find you most changeable are the moments when you can most change yourself: the new city, the new job, the new semester, the Monday after everything shifted. Habits are at their weakest exactly when life is in motion. The companies know this and plan around your transitions. The opportunity is to get there first, and use the window on purpose.

Pole's model also illustrates something easy to miss: it worked not by watching what people said but by watching what they repeatedly did. Surveys are full of intentions; receipts are full of truth. A person's routines are a biography written in behaviour — more honest than memory, more predictive than promises. That is why the same science that lets a retailer forecast a pregnancy lets a coach forecast which player will crack under pressure, and why it can show you, if you keep simple records of your own days, patterns in your life that your self-image has politely concealed. The data of a life is its habits.

Psychologists who study life transitions confirm the opportunity from the other side. In studies of people who have recently moved house, researchers find that habits — even strong ones, like cycling to work or recycling — wobble when the environment changes, because the cues that sustained them have been stripped away. For a few weeks, behaviour is unusually negotiable, driven by conscious intention rather than context. Then new cues settle, new loops harden, and the window closes. Everyone passes through such windows: the first month in a new school, job, city or relationship is a period when you are, briefly, the chief executive of your own behaviour rather than its employee. Most people let the window close on its own. The argument of this book is that you can climb through it on purpose.

CHAPTER 9
Saddleback and the Power of Small Wins

In 1979, a young pastor named Rick Warren arrived in Orange County, California, with a dream of building a church for people who did not go to church. He had no building, no congregation and almost no money — he and his wife had a few hundred dollars between them. He went door to door asking residents what would keep them away from a church, and then built Saddleback Church around their answers: practical sermons, contemporary music, a campus that felt more like a community centre than a cathedral. Within a few decades, Saddleback had grown into one of the largest churches in the world, drawing tens of thousands of worshippers each week to a sprawling campus and satellites around the globe.

Growth on that scale creates an obvious problem. A stadium crowd is not a community; people can attend for years and remain anonymous, unconnected and unchanged. Warren's answer drew, consciously or not, on the science of habits. Saddleback engineered a pathway of belonging built from small, concrete steps. Newcomers were invited to a class. The class led to a commitment. And crucially, everyone was steered into a small group — a handful of families meeting in someone's living room to study, eat and share their lives. In those rooms, faith stopped being a Sunday routine performed in a crowd and became a web of weekday habits performed among friends.

The small groups did the heavy lifting of behaviour change. Members prayed together, confessed struggles, celebrated progress and noticed absences. When someone wanted to lose weight, repair a marriage or break an addiction, the group supplied what researchers of habit change keep identifying as essential: belief, carried by other people. It is one thing to tell yourself that change is possible at midnight, alone, after a failure. It is another to sit in a room with eight people who know your story, expect you on Thursday, and include two or three who have already done the thing you are attempting. The cue, the routine and the reward were all redesigned socially: the cue became the group's rhythm, the routine became showing up and being honest, and the reward became belonging.

Warren also understood small wins. Saddleback's famous health campaign began when Warren, baptising hundreds of converts by immersion one season, realised how heavy his congregation — himself included — had become. Rather than demanding a total life overhaul, the church launched a year-long programme built on modest, measurable steps: slightly better food choices, a little more movement, group accountability. Tens of thousands participated; collectively they lost hundreds of thousands of pounds. The genius of the design was that nobody was asked to become a different person on day one. They were asked to win small, this week, with witnesses.

Psychologists have a name for why this works. Small wins are not merely miniature versions of big victories; they have an influence out of proportion to their size, because they convince the brain that change is real and thereby recruit effort toward the next step. A small win reorganises how a situation is perceived: obstacles shrink, confidence compounds, and the next routine becomes easier to start. This is the same mechanism that made Paul O'Neill's safety programme powerful and the food journal transformative. Momentum, once established, does much of the work that willpower cannot.

The Saddleback story completes a triangle that runs through this book. Habits live in individuals, but they are sustained by communities and institutions. Dungy's players changed inside a team. Alcoholics change inside a fellowship. Baristas changed inside a company that scripted their routines. At Saddleback, an institution deliberately built the social architecture — small groups, graduated commitments, celebrated small wins — that makes personal change durable. If you are trying to change a habit and failing alone, the lesson is not that you are weak. It may be that you are missing your living room: a handful of people whose expectations, rhythms and belief can carry your new routine through the weeks when your own motivation cannot.

The phrase "small wins" comes from the organisational psychologist Karl Weick, who argued that people facing large problems often become paralysed by their scale, and that the cure is to recast the problem at a size where success is achievable. A win at that scale is not trivial: it lowers arousal, restores a sense of control, and — crucially — attracts allies, because success is easier to join than struggle. Movements, companies and congregations that grow tend to be machines for manufacturing such wins in sequence. Saddleback's health programme followed the logic precisely, down to recruiting respected doctors to design it and small groups to witness it. Nobody was asked to become healthy. They were asked to drink water instead of soda this week, walk with their group on Saturday, and report back. Then the wins began to stack.

Stack enough of them and something qualitative changes: identity. Psychologists distinguish between outcome goals — lose ten pounds, save a sum — and identity goals: become the kind of person who eats carefully, who provides for the future. Small wins are the evidence from which a new identity is assembled. Each kept promise is a vote for the person you are becoming, and votes accumulate into a verdict. This is why the most durable habit changes in these stories — at Alcoa, at Starbucks, at Saddleback — always end with people describing themselves differently. The routines changed first. The story about the self followed behind, and then guarded the routines.

CHAPTER 10
The Neurology of Free Will

One night, a woman named Angie Bachmann sat down at a slot machine in a casino and did not get up for many hours. She had begun gambling during a lonely period of her life, when her children were growing older and her days felt empty, and at first it was harmless entertainment. The casino, however, understood habits better than she did. It gave her a loyalty card that tracked her play, sent her free rooms and meals, and assigned her a host who called with invitations. Her cues multiplied — boredom, the mail, the drive past the exit — and her routines deepened. Eventually she lost her family's savings, then money she borrowed, then the inheritance she had received from her parents: in total, close to a million dollars. When she finally stopped, ruined, she sued the casino, arguing in essence that her habits had made her do it — that a brain hijacked by craving is not a free brain, and responsibility should lie with those who engineered the trap.

Her case forces the hardest question in the science of habits: if so much of behaviour is automatic, where does responsibility live? The law, in her case, answered bluntly — the casino won; the court held that she, not the slot machines, bore responsibility for her losses. But the more interesting answer comes from comparing her story with another: that of a man who, one night, killed his wife in his sleep. He suffered from a sleep disorder in which the brain's normal paralysis during dreaming fails, and he acted out a nightmare with catastrophic results. He was acquitted, because during sleep the conscious mind is genuinely absent; there was, in a meaningful sense, nobody home to choose.

The difference between the two cases is the difference between a habit and a reflex. The sleepwalker could not have intervened at any point — no cue reached a waking mind. Bachmann's gambling, by contrast, passed through thousands of moments where awareness was possible: the drive, the door, the first bet, the decision to continue. A habit is automatic, but it is not compulsory. Once you know a loop exists — once you can name its cue and its reward — a gap opens between trigger and routine, and in that gap, choice becomes possible again. Neurologists make a similar point: the first step in every successful treatment of destructive habits is the patient's recognition of the pattern, because recognition is what converts an invisible reflex into a visible object the mind can work on.

This is not a comfortable doctrine, and it cuts in two directions. It means the gambler cannot wholly blame the casino — yet it also means the casino's designers, who deliberately built cues and rewards to bypass deliberation, cannot wholly escape blame either. Responsibility, in a world of engineered habits, is shared in proportion to knowledge. The more clearly a person understands their own loops, the more responsible they become for redesigning them. And the more clearly an institution understands the loops it builds into other people's lives, the more responsible it is for what those loops do.

Bachmann herself, in later interviews, came to a version of this view. She stopped gambling — not through the lawsuit, but through the slower work of recognising her cues, avoiding the environments saturated with them, and building a life with fewer empty hours in it. Her story ends less dramatically than it began, which is precisely the point. Freedom, in the science of habits, rarely looks like a single heroic act of will. It looks like awareness, repeated: noticing the cue earlier and earlier, widening the gap between trigger and routine, and using that gap, again and again, until the new pattern becomes the automatic one. We are not responsible for the first craving that rises in us. We are responsible for what we build around it.

History offers a natural experiment that sharpens the point. During the Vietnam War, heroin use among American soldiers was rampant — the drug was cheap, potent and everywhere, and a large share of enlisted men used it, many becoming addicted. Officials braced for an epidemic of addiction when the troops came home. It never arrived. Follow-up studies found that the overwhelming majority of soldiers who had been addicted in Vietnam simply stopped when they returned, most without treatment. Their cues — the jungle, the stress, the availability, the companions — had been left on another continent. The loop starved in a new environment. The small minority who did relapse tended to be those whose home lives reproduced the old conditions. Few findings in addiction science are more hopeful, or more instructive: change the cues comprehensively enough, and even a powerful chemical habit can lose its grip.

Set the two stories side by side and the doctrine becomes clear. Bachmann's environment was deliberately saturated with cues, and she walked into it again and again; the soldiers' environment was replaced wholesale, and the habit withered. Neither outcome was decided by the strength of anyone's character in a single moment of temptation. Both were decided by architecture — by what surrounded the person in the thousands of moments before temptation arrived. That is why this book treats habit change as design rather than combat. You are not only the soldier in your own story. You are also, if you choose to be, the one who decides where the war is fought.

CHAPTER 11
Redesigning a Life

By now the pieces of the puzzle are on the table. Habits run on a loop of cue, routine and reward, powered by craving. They cannot be deleted, but they can be changed — by keeping the cue and the reward and swapping the routine. Some habits are keystones that reorganise everything around them. Willpower is a muscle that tires and trains, and it can be converted into habit through plans made in advance. Organisations and communities run on the same machinery, and they change in moments of urgency, inside groups that supply belief, one small win at a time. The remaining question is the practical one: how do you actually redesign a habit in an ordinary life, starting from wherever you are?

The first move is to stop fighting the whole behaviour and start investigating it. Most people attack their habits at the level of the routine — I must stop snacking, stop scrolling, stop snapping — which is the one part of the loop least accessible to direct control, because by the time the routine is running, the craving is already driving. The investigator's approach is quieter. For a few days, you simply observe. When does the urge arrive? Where are you? Who is with you? What just happened? What feeling came first? The behaviour you are studying has been invisible to you precisely because it is automatic; observation is how it becomes visible, and visibility is the precondition of everything else.

Consider the afternoon biscuit habit, a classic of office life. On the surface it is about hunger or greed. Investigated honestly, it often turns out to be something else: the break in concentration at three o'clock, the walk past the kitchen, the colleague who always joins, the brief social warmth and the sugar lift together. The real reward might be distraction, or company, or a pause — and each diagnosis implies a different replacement routine. If the reward is a break, a short walk outside may deliver it. If it is company, a standing chat with a friend may deliver it. If it is genuinely hunger, an apple kept in the desk drawer may deliver it. People who misdiagnose the reward choose replacement routines that do not satisfy the craving, and then conclude, wrongly, that they lack discipline.

Environment design does much of the remaining work. Cues live in places: the phone on the pillow manufactures the midnight scroll; the running shoes by the door manufacture the morning run. Since cues trigger loops whether we approve or not, the wise response is gardening rather than warfare — plant cues for the habits you want and uproot cues for the ones you do not. Companies do this to us constantly, as Target demonstrated. Doing it deliberately, to ourselves, on our own behalf, is simply reclaiming the machinery.

It also helps to shrink the change until it cannot fail. The research on small wins says that a modest routine performed daily rewires expectations faster than an ambitious routine performed twice. Ten minutes of reading beats the abandoned plan to read an hour. One page of writing beats the novel that exists only as guilt. This is not lowering standards; it is sequencing them. The keystone habit you can actually keep this month is worth more than the perfect system you will quit by Friday, because the kept habit compounds — in skill, in identity, and in the belief that you are a person whose plans for themselves come true.

Finally, expect the old loop to wait for you. It will still be there on the stressful day, at the old café, in the old neighbourhood, months after you thought it was gone — because it was never erased, only outcompeted. Relapse, in this science, is not proof that change failed; it is information about a cue you had not mapped. The people who change for good are rarely the ones who never slip. They are the ones who treat each slip as data, adjust the plan, recruit their people, and begin again a little wiser about their own machinery. That is what redesign looks like from the inside: not a battle won once, but a system steadily improved.

One more tool makes the investigation stick: writing it down. Every community in this book that changed successfully kept some form of record — Alcoa's injury reports, the dieter's food journal, the sponsor's nightly call, Saddleback's group check-ins. A simple notebook beside the loop you are studying — time, place, feeling, what you did, what you actually wanted — converts vague self-criticism into usable evidence within a fortnight. People resist this step because it feels like homework. It is better understood as instrumentation. A pilot does not apologise for the altimeter. Your notebook is how you see the weather inside your own behaviour, and nobody redesigns what they cannot see.

And begin tonight, at whatever scale tonight allows. Not next month, when life calms down — life is the medium habits grow in, and it does not calm down. The redesigned life is not built in a retreat from the ordinary one. It is built inside it: one cue moved, one routine rehearsed, one reward honestly named, one small win witnessed by people who are pulling for you. That is the whole method, practised at the scale of a single day.

CHAPTER 12
A Framework for Changing Habits

Everything in this book converges on a method, and it is fitting to end by stating it plainly, as a framework you can apply to any habit in your life. It has four steps. They are simple to describe and require patience to perform, because you are doing fieldwork on the most elusive subject there is: yourself.

The first step is to identify the routine. Choose one habit you want to change and name its routine precisely — not "I eat badly" but "at three in the afternoon, I walk to the kitchen and eat biscuits while standing at the counter." Precision matters because vague routines cannot be redesigned. If the habit is one you want to build rather than break, the same step applies in mirror image: define exactly the behaviour you want to make automatic, small enough to perform on your worst day.

The second step is to experiment with rewards. The routine exists because it delivers something, and your first guess about what that something is will often be wrong. So test alternatives the way a scientist would. On successive days, when the cue arrives, give yourself different candidate rewards: take a walk, talk to a friend, make tea, eat an apple, rest for ten minutes. After each experiment, set a timer for fifteen minutes and ask the only question that matters: do I still crave the old routine? If the craving is gone, you have found a reward that satisfies the loop. If it persists, cross that candidate off and test another. Within a week or two of honest experiments, the true reward usually stands revealed — and it is frequently not pleasure but relief: from boredom, fatigue, loneliness, anxiety or the need for a pause.

The third step is to isolate the cue. Research shows that almost all habit cues fall into five categories: location, time, emotional state, other people, and the action that immediately precedes the routine. So each time the urge strikes during your experiments, write down five things: where you are, what time it is, how you feel, who else is around, and what you were just doing. After several days, the pattern surfaces. Perhaps the urge arrives at the same hour regardless of place — the cue is time. Perhaps it arrives only when a particular colleague appears, or only after checking email, or only when your energy dips. You are looking for the one factor that is present every time, the true trigger of the loop.

The fourth step is to have a plan. Once you know the cue and the reward, you can write the new loop in advance, in the if-then form that Starbucks taught its baristas: when the cue occurs, I will perform this new routine, which delivers the reward I actually need. Write it down. Rehearse it mentally. Tell your people — the small group whose belief will carry you through the flat weeks. Then expect imperfection: the plan will fail on some days, especially early, and each failure is data about a cue you missed or a reward you misjudged. Adjust, and run the experiment again.

It is worth being honest about the timescale. Habits formed over years do not rewire in a weekend; studies of habit formation suggest new routines take weeks to months of repetition before they feel automatic, and the range between individuals is wide. But the direction of travel is what matters, and it begins with a shift in how you see yourself: not as a battleground between virtue and weakness, but as a designer working with machinery that has rules. Eugene Pauly, whose damaged brain opened this book, could not remember his way home — yet his habits carried him there, every day, without fail. The machinery is that faithful. It will carry any of us, just as reliably, along whatever loops we build for it. The work of a life is choosing the loops on purpose.

How long should you expect the work to take? When researchers at University College London tracked people building new habits — drinking water at lunch, doing sit-ups after breakfast — they found that automaticity arrived, on average, after about sixty-six days, with a wide range: some habits clicked in under three weeks, others took the better part of a year. Missing a single day did not measurably damage the process. That last finding may be the most merciful in the literature. The plan does not need to be perfect. It needs to be resumed.

Look back across the stories in this book and the framework is everywhere, wearing different clothes. Dungy identified his players' cues and rewards and installed new routines between them. O'Neill found a keystone habit and let small wins reorganise a corporation. Starbucks wrote if-then plans so willpower could rest. A hospital rewrote its truces in a moment of crisis. A congregation built the groups that make belief — and therefore change — contagious. And one woman who lost nearly everything to a slot machine rebuilt her life the slow way: by knowing her cues, starving them, and filling her hours with better rewards. None of these people escaped the habit loop. None of us can. But each of them learned to see it — and seeing it, to build with it rather than be built by it. That is the entire invitation of this book: pick one loop, study it honestly, redesign it patiently, and let the machinery that once ran you by default begin, at last, to run in the direction you chose.
