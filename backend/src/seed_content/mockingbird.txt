To Kill a Mockingbird
Harper Lee

CHAPTER 1
The Town That Time Forgot

When I look back on the summer everything changed, I do not begin with a courtroom or a gun or the dark road home from a school pageant. I begin with heat lying over Maycomb like a hand, with red dust softening the edges of the streets, and with my brother Jem four years older and certain he knew the names of all our dangers. Our father, Atticus Finch, practiced law in a small office off the square. He was not young, not especially strong in the ways boys admire, and not interested in pretending otherwise. He read in the evenings, spoke to everyone with the same courtesy, and believed a person ought to be understood from the inside before being judged from the porch.

Maycomb was an old Alabama town, tired in the way of places that have survived by habit. The courthouse sagged in the middle of the square. Stores opened slowly. People moved as if the air itself had weight. In rainy weather the streets turned to red slop; in dry weather the collards and courthouse steps gathered dust. There was nowhere particular to go and no hurry in getting there, which suited children until curiosity made its own weather.

Our house stood on the main residential street, close enough to the Radley place for it to trouble our imaginations and far enough away for us to claim bravery. Calpurnia kept our household from disorder after our mother died. She was firm, warm in the manner of weathered wood, and able to silence Jem and me with one look over her spectacles. Atticus trusted her, which taught us before we had words for it that worth was not announced by a person's station.

The Radley house leaned at the end of our street, shuttered and sunken, its yard overgrown and its porch seldom swept by any hand we saw. Mr. Radley had been a stern man; after him came his son Nathan, who kept the same closed doors. But it was the other son, Arthur, whom the town had turned into a legend. As a boy he had fallen in with the wrong companions and caused enough trouble for his father to promise the judge he would keep Arthur at home. After that the doors shut on him for years.

Children enlarged what adults whispered. Arthur Radley became Boo, a figure who ate raw animals, froze flowers with a breath, and watched from behind curtains while the town slept. Jem, who enjoyed authority in these matters, described him tall and scarred, with yellow teeth and eyes that popped. Dill would later demand proof. At the time I believed every word and resented the fear that made me believe it.

Negro families lived beyond the town's easy center, near the church and cabins by the dump, and though our childish map was mostly white porches and familiar names, Maycomb's true boundaries were sharper than geography. People knew who belonged where, who could speak first, whose word would be believed, and whose would be weighed against a prejudice older than any of us. I did not yet have language for that design. I only knew that Atticus sometimes grew quiet at the supper table when the talk turned mean, and that Calpurnia's face could close like a door.

Miss Maudie Atkinson, across the street, baked cakes for us and tended flowers with cheerful ferocity. She did not gossip in the usual currency. When Jem repeated some piece of Radley lore, she reminded us that Arthur Radley had been a polite boy once and that a house could hold sorrow without holding a monster. It was the first hint that stories told often enough can become a cage for the person inside them.

Our summer boundaries were the houses of neighbors we knew and the Radley fence we feared to cross. We played in the yard, made dramas from books and boredom, and treated the Radley porch as the edge of the known world. Jem insisted the bravest act would be to touch the house. I insisted bravery was not the same as foolishness, though my voice shook when I said it.

Atticus, coming home with his briefcase, would find us mid-argument and settle matters by asking whether we would enjoy being tormented for the amusement of strangers. We said no, of course. He told us to leave the Radleys to their privacy. It sounded simple, almost too simple for the size of our fascination. Only later did I learn that nearly every decent rule Atticus gave us was simple enough for a child to repeat and hard enough for a town to fail.

That spring I was six, Jem was ten, and our world seemed no larger than a street, a schoolyard, and a shuttered house. We thought courage meant daring the dark. We thought evil wore a face we could point to. Maycomb would spend the next two years correcting us, sometimes gently, sometimes with a cruelty that still wakes me when the house is quiet. It began, as most reckonings do, with a stranger arriving for the summer and asking why we did not simply go and see for ourselves.

CHAPTER 2
The Boy From Meridian

Dill came to Maycomb in the early summer, small for his age and white-haired, with a mind that moved faster than his legs. His full name was Charles Baker Harris, and he stayed with his aunt, Miss Rachel Haverford, next door. He had seen a picture show in Meridian and carried its wonders into our yard like contraband. Where Jem and I had exhausted our repertoire of dramas, Dill arrived with plots, disguises, escapes, and the serene confidence that any fence could be climbed if the story required it.

He was seven, nearly, and could read earlier than I could, which annoyed me until his company proved more valuable than my pride. He told us about Dracula, about snow, about trains, about a father who was not dead but might as well have been for all the use he was to Dill. Beneath the bragging was a loneliness I recognized only years later. Dill invented worlds because the real one had a habit of sending him away.

Our games that summer were built around books we had read and films Dill described. We became outlaws, explorers, and doomed heroes beneath the chinaberry trees. Jem directed; Dill improved; I demanded roles better than the ones usually assigned to girls in our productions and complained when tree houses and Indian-heads were treated as male property. The Radley place watched us from the end of the street, or we imagined it did, which came to the same thing in the economy of childhood.

It was Dill who made Boo Radley into a project. Rumors were not enough for him. He wanted evidence, a face at the window, a shadow, some sign that the legend breathed. Jem explained the history in the solemn tones of a courthouse guide: the closed shutters, the father who had kept his son from the reform school by promising to keep him home, the scissors story whispered by people who should have known better than to repeat it to children. Dill listened with shining eyes. "If he comes out," he said, "what'll he do?" Jem said he would probably kill us. Dill considered this a satisfactory beginning.

The dare took shape slowly. First we only ventured nearer the fence. Then we spat on the gate to make it stop squeaking in our imaginations. Then Dill bet Jem The Gray Ghost against two Tom Swifts that Jem would not touch the house. Jem valued books and pride in roughly equal measure. He walked with elaborate casualness to the Radley gate, paused while my heart battered my ribs, ran up the steps, slapped the wall, and fled as if the porch itself might open.

Nothing happened. No shutter moved. No voice called out. Yet as we caught our breath in our own yard, I thought I saw an inside shutter stir, no more than a tremor of light along the edge. I said nothing at first because Jem would have called it invention and Dill would have turned it into a campaign. But the tremor stayed with me. Behind that shutter was not the monster of our theater. It was a person who had noticed children and chosen, for reasons we could not know, to remain unseen.

After that, Dill's curiosity sharpened. He devised schemes involving notes on fishing poles, peeking through loose shutters, and knocking at the back door with offers of ice cream. Jem pretended to be reluctant, which in Jem meant he was already planning. I followed because being left behind was a worse fear than Boo Radley, a truth about childhood that explains more of my conduct that summer than bravery does.

Atticus caught the direction of our interest and warned us again. He did not raise his voice. He rarely needed to. He said the Radleys had a right to their own way of living and that tormenting a man who had done us no harm was neither fair nor mannerly. Dill, new to Atticus's methods, tried charm. Atticus received it politely and remained unmoved. We promised to behave, in the limited sense that we promised to be more careful about being observed.

School approached with its own dread. I had waited to go the way other children wait for a fair, imagining desks, books, and the dignity of being among people who read. Dill returned to Meridian, promising to marry me in a letter whose spelling was more confident than its grammar. Jem walked me toward the schoolyard on the first morning, instructing me not to embarrass him by mentioning our plays or asking teachers questions meant for home.

I did not know that the classroom would teach me my first lesson in how good intentions collide with other people's worlds. Nor did I know that Boo Radley, whom we were trying to lure into the open, would spend the coming seasons leaving signs that he had been watching us with an entirely different intention. We thought we were hunting a ghost. We were being noticed by a neighbor.


CHAPTER 3
Miss Caroline's Classroom

I entered first grade believing school would reward the one accomplishment I was proudest of: I could read. I do not remember learning in the ordinary way children are taught, letter by letter at a desk. Atticus had read to us at night, following the words with his finger, and somewhere between his voice and the printed page the sounds unlocked themselves. By the time Miss Caroline Fisher stood before the class, I could manage newspapers, hymnbooks, and most of the stories in our reader. I expected praise. I received a problem.

Miss Caroline was young, from Winston County, and new to Maycomb's arrangements. She had a system learned in college and a faith that the class would conform to it if she were brisk enough. She wrote the alphabet on the board, pointed to me, and asked whether I knew the letters. I read them. Then I read a passage. Her expression altered from pleasure to concern, as if I had produced a card trick during arithmetic.

She told me to inform my father that he should stop teaching me because he did not know how. I was too astonished to be properly insulted. Atticus, in my experience, knew how to do a great many things, including some he wisely declined to perform. At recess Jem found me near tears and offered the consolation that she was introducing a new method and probably meant no harm. It was generous of him, and almost comforting, until the afternoon brought Walter Cunningham into my difficulties.

Walter was a Cunningham from Old Sarum, clean in a shirt that had been mended into respectability and thin in the way of children whose families worked hard and ate what the land allowed. When Miss Caroline offered him a quarter for lunch, promising he could repay her tomorrow, he refused with a patience that baffled her. She insisted. He explained, in few words, that he could not take what he could not pay back. The Cunninghams were poor, but their poverty had rules, and one of them was that a debt you cannot honor is not accepted.

I tried to translate. "You're shamin' him," I said, meaning that everyone in the room except our teacher understood the code. Miss Caroline heard defiance instead of explanation. She rapped my palm with a ruler, ordered me to stand in the corner, and completed my education for the day by making an example of the child who had arrived already ruined by reading.

At lunch I took my resentment out on Walter in the schoolyard, because he was near and because shame seeks a smaller target. Jem separated us and, to my amazement, invited Walter home to dinner. At the table Walter poured syrup over his food until Atticus's glance taught me silence more effectively than any scolding. Calpurnia finished the lesson in the kitchen. She told me that company did not have to eat as I did to deserve courtesy, and that my mouth would get me into more trouble than my fists if I did not learn to govern both.

That afternoon Burris Ewell provided a different lesson. He came to school once a year, filthy, insolent, and proud of a system in which the law bent because pursuing his family cost more than surrendering. When Miss Caroline recoiled from a creature in his hair and suggested he wash before returning, Burris informed her that he would not be returning until the next first day. He left insulting her with the ease of a boy repeating the manners of his house.

I went home convinced that school was an institution designed to prevent learning. Atticus listened while I made my case for staying away as Burris did, with a legal arrangement suited to my needs. Then he offered a bargain and a piece of advice I have carried all my life. If I agreed to go to school, we would go on reading together at night. As for Miss Caroline, he said I would understand people better if I learned to step into their skin and move around in it before judging. It was not the phrase he used that mattered most. It was the discipline beneath it: pause, imagine, withhold the verdict.

I returned to school because a bargain with Atticus was a serious thing. I also returned because Jem, for all his bossiness, had crossed into a world of older boys and new loyalties, and I was not ready to be left entirely behind. The classroom remained bewildering. But I had begun to suspect that education was not only what happened at a desk. Sometimes it was a ruler across the palm, a guest drowning his dinner in syrup, and a father asking his daughter to practice mercy before she practiced contempt.

CHAPTER 4
Treasures in the Knot-Hole

The Radley oak stood at the corner near the school, old enough to have its own weather and broad enough to shade a generation of children hurrying past with their eyes averted. One afternoon, coming home alone, I noticed something bright in a knot-hole at about the height of my shoulder. It was chewing gum, wrapped as neatly as a secret. I should have left it. I should have considered poison, traps, and every story Jem had ever told. Instead I took it home, and when I did not die, Jem's outrage turned quickly to envy.

A few days later we found two pennies, polished and old, pressed into a small box in the same hole. They were Indian-head pennies, the kind people kept for luck. Jem turned them over with the gravity of a banker. Someone was leaving things where children passing the Radley place would find them. The gifts were not valuable in money. They were valuable because they contradicted the legend. Monsters did not usually concern themselves with gum and luck.

That summer Dill returned and our Radley campaign resumed with more elaborate theater. We invented a game in which Jem played Mr. Radley, I played Mrs. Radley when required, and Dill took the part of Boo with a sheet and great dedication. We enacted the scissors story, the trial, the confinement, and every variation our imaginations could wring from gossip. Atticus stopped on the sidewalk one day and asked what drama we were performing. Jem, suddenly formal, said it was just a game. Atticus looked at the sheet, at the scissors, at our faces, and advised us to stop tormenting that man. He did not take the props. He did something worse: he made us hear ourselves.

We promised, and in the manner of children we altered the names and continued in secret for a while. But the knot-hole gifts had changed the emotional weather. It is difficult to make a villain of someone who may be leaving you treasures. Dill still wanted a sighting. Jem still wanted mastery over fear. I began to want an explanation that did not require anyone to be a monster.

The night raid was Dill's masterpiece and our collective folly. On his last evening before returning to Meridian, we decided to peek through a loose shutter at the back of the Radley house. The moon gave little help. The fence complained. A shadow crossed the porch, not the theatrical shadow of our games but a man's shape, still and close. Then came the roar of a shotgun and the kind of fear that makes the body move before the mind has finished its sentence.

Jem lost his pants to the fence in our escape. It would have been comic if terror had left room for comedy. Later, with the neighborhood awake and Mr. Nathan Radley explaining that he had fired at a prowler in his collard patch, Jem had to return for them. Atticus's questions were mild, which made them harder to answer. Jem invented a story about strip poker by the fishpool, and I learned that my brother could lie with a completeness I had not suspected when the alternative was admitting we had gone hunting Boo.

He came back near dawn with the pants folded across his arm and a strangeness in his face he would not explain until later. They had not been caught on the fence as he left them, tangled and torn. Someone had freed them, mended them clumsily, and laid them over the fence like clothes set out for a child. Jem kept that fact between us for a time. It was too large for Dill's theater and too tender for ridicule.

In the autumn, the knot-hole yielded more gifts: twine, a spelling medal, a watch that did not run, and two small figures carved from soap, a boy and a girl in a dress. Jem and I stared at those figures with a recognition we were almost afraid to name. Someone knew us. Someone had watched long enough to shape our likenesses with care. We wrote a note of thanks and put it in the hole the next day, planning gratitude like a ceremony.

Nathan Radley filled the knot-hole with cement. He said the tree was dying. Atticus, when Jem asked, said the tree looked healthy enough and that Mr. Radley would know his own business best. Jem stood on the porch that evening and cried without letting me see his face if he could help it. I understood then that the gifts had been a conversation, and the cement was an answer from a world that did not approve of it. Someone inside the shuttered house had reached toward us in the only language left to him, and someone else had sealed the door.


CHAPTER 5
Snow, and a House Afire

Winter came to Maycomb as an insult nobody had ordered. I woke to a world whitened at the edges and concluded, in the logic of an Alabama child, that some catastrophe had occurred. Jem was more practical and more delighted. Snow in our town was rare enough to close school and send children into yards with the solemn purpose of people who may never see the phenomenon again. We borrowed what snow the earth and Miss Maudie's yard could spare and built a figure that began as mud and ended, with a snow skin, resembling Mr. Avery more than we intended.

Atticus surveyed our creation and suggested we disguise the likeness before our neighbor took offense. We gave the snowman Miss Maudie's hat and hedge clippers, which improved the joke and worsened the resemblance in a different direction. It was one of those bright, brief pleasures that childhood stores against later darkness. By nightfall the cold had sharpened. Fires were built up in grates all over town, and people went to bed with the uneasy feeling that a Southern house, framed for heat, does not know what to do with freezing air.

I woke to Atticus's hand on my shoulder and the smell of smoke. Miss Maudie's house was burning. The sky beyond our street had turned the color of a struck match. Neighbors poured into the road in nightclothes and coats, carrying furniture, calling instructions, forming the kind of bucket line that appears when a town remembers it is a town. Atticus sent Jem and me to stand by the Radley gate, out of the way and where he could see us.

We stood in the cold and watched Miss Maudie's home surrender room by room. The fire made giants of ordinary men. Mr. Avery, who had warned us that the weather was changing because of disobedient children, got himself stuck in an upstairs window trying to save a mattress and had to be rescued with more embarrassment than injury. The fire truck, ill-suited to the cold, arrived with the reluctance of an old mule. By dawn the house was a blackened shell and the snowman had melted into a muddy ghost.

Miss Maudie faced the ruin with a composure that shamed my tears. She spoke of rebuilding smaller, with more room for her flowers. She hated that house, she said, in the tone of someone refusing to let loss choose her manners. When she saw my face she told me not to worry; she had always wanted a smaller house anyway. I knew adults sometimes lied to spare children. This felt different. It felt like courage practiced at breakfast.

Only when we were home did Atticus notice the blanket around my shoulders. It was not ours. Jem went pale and began, in a rush, to confess everything: the knot-hole, the gifts, the pants, the night raid, all the secrets spilling because the blanket had told on us more surely than any witness. Atticus listened without interrupting. Someone had stood behind me in the cold, close enough to wrap a blanket around a shivering child, and had withdrawn before I turned. Boo Radley had been there among the neighbors, unseen as usual, kind in the only way his life allowed.

Atticus told Jem that we would not return the blanket in a way that embarrassed its giver. Some debts, he implied, are paid by discretion. I thought of the shutter trembling, the carved soap figures, the mended pants. The pattern was no longer a mystery to be solved but a person to be considered. Our monster had hands, and he used them gently.

The fire left Miss Maudie homeless but not defeated, and it left me with a new fear: not of Boo, but of how wrong we had been while feeling so certain. Jem, who had carried the pants secret like a stone, seemed lighter and more thoughtful after confessing. He did not stop being a boy who loved dares. But he began to understand that the Radley story might be less about what Arthur Radley had done to the town than about what the town had done to Arthur Radley, year after year, by refusing to let him be merely a man behind a door.

In the days after the fire, neighbors carried casseroles, spare blankets, and opinions to Miss Stephanie Crawford's porch, where Miss Maudie stayed while she decided what to rebuild. Atticus helped her read the insurance papers at our kitchen table, and she listened with her chin up, interrupting only to say that a house was lumber and nails until people made more of it. I watched her choose jokes over bitterness and understood that dignity is not the absence of loss. Sometimes it is the refusal to hand loss the final word. Jem said she was game. I thought she was something better: she was undefeated without pretending she had not been burned.

CHAPTER 6
The Mad Dog and the Mockingbird

February brought a stillness that even children could feel. Jem was nearly twelve and passing through a season of moods; I was learning that growing older did not always mean growing easier to live with. One Saturday we saw Tim Johnson, the Finch family's familiar liver-colored setter, coming down the street in a way that made the hair rise on my neck. He moved with a crooked patience, head low, body listing, as if the road were tilting under him. Jem knew before I did. Mad dog. The words emptied the street faster than a shout.

Calpurnia acted with the speed of a general. She got us inside, telephoned Atticus at his office, warned Miss Rachel and the Radley household by sending word up the street, and kept her body between us and the window when curiosity pulled us toward the glass. I remember her hand, firm on my shoulder, and the sudden knowledge that the woman who ran our kitchen could also run an emergency without wasting a syllable.

Atticus arrived with Sheriff Heck Tate in a car that seemed too slow for the size of my fear. The street was empty. Doors were shut. Tim Johnson came on, poor creature, sick past saving and dangerous to anything in his path. Mr. Tate held his rifle and then, to my astonishment, handed it to Atticus. He said his own shot might miss by enough to matter and that Atticus had once been the surest shot in the county. My father, who did not hunt, who had never boasted of any skill with a gun, took the rifle as if it were a burden he had hoped not to lift again.

He stepped into the street alone. He pushed his glasses up, and in the stillness I saw a man I did not know assemble himself inside the father I did. The shot cracked the afternoon. Tim Johnson folded before he reached the Radley gate. Jem and I stood stunned by the double revelation: the dog was dead, and Atticus had been hiding a talent the way other men hide a fault.

Miss Maudie explained later, with satisfaction, that Atticus had been called One-Shot Finch in his youth. She also told us why he did not make a gospel of it. A man with a steady hand and a rifle has an unfair advantage over most living things; Atticus, she said, had put his gun down when he understood that advantage and did not pick it up for pride. Civilized in his heart, she called him. I turned the phrase over for days.

Jem was transformed by pride. He walked taller, spoke of Atticus with new reverence, and declared he would not trade him for any father who could throw farther or fight better. I was slower. I had wanted Atticus to be remarkable in ways that could be displayed at school. Now I was confronted by the possibility that the most remarkable thing about him was restraint: power kept sheathed, skill without appetite, strength that did not need an audience.

It was around this time that Atticus gave us his rule about mockingbirds. We had air rifles, and with them the usual temptations of boys and girls with targets. He would not teach us to shoot, leaving that to Uncle Jack, but he set a boundary we never forgot. Bluejays could be pests if we must, though he doubted our aim; mockingbirds were different. They made music and harmed no one. To kill one would be a sin, not because a bird is sacred in itself, but because cruelty toward the harmless is a particular kind of wrong.

Miss Maudie filled in the edges of the lesson. Mockingbirds do not eat gardens or nest in corncribs; they sing their hearts out for us. That is why, she said, it is wrong to kill them. I thought of Boo Radley behind his shutter, of gifts left in a tree, of a blanket placed around my shoulders in the cold. The town had many ways of shooting at what it did not understand: gossip, law, custom, averted eyes. Some weapons made less noise than a rifle. Some wounds took longer to show.

Jem, still glowing from the mad dog, began to treat Atticus's past as a legend equal to any in our games. I began to see the present more clearly. The same man who could drop a rabid dog at a distance had chosen a life of books, courtesy, and law. That choice was about to be tested by a case that would divide Maycomb more deeply than any mad animal on a February road. Atticus knew it was coming. We children only knew that the adults' voices had begun to lower when we entered rooms.


That night I asked Atticus why he had never told us he could shoot. He said a gift that can kill is not the sort of thing a man parades before his children, and that he hoped we would value him for what he did every day rather than for one afternoon in an empty street. I did not fully understand him then, but I remembered the steadiness of his voice long after I forgot the sound of the shot.


CHAPTER 7
The Camellia and the Alarm Clock

Mrs. Henry Lafayette Dubose lived two doors up, in a house whose porch we passed with the speed of travelers crossing hostile country. She was very old, very ill, and famous for a tongue that could strip paint. Her face, when she was wheeled onto the porch, had the folded look of someone in constant pain, though at the time I considered only how perfectly she seemed designed to ruin a child's afternoon. She criticized our clothes, our manners, our father's work, and the general decline of the Finch family since some better generation.

Jem usually bore her insults with a tight jaw. I was less disciplined. One day, after she had aimed her remarks at Atticus and the company he kept in his practice, Jem's patience broke. On our way back from town he seized my baton, charged into her yard, and chopped the heads off her camellia bushes with the fury of a small executioner. White and red blossoms fell like a massacre in a garden. I watched, thrilled and horrified, knowing the pleasure would be billed to us at a rate we could not yet calculate.

Atticus was waiting when we got home. He did not shout. He sent Jem back to apologize and to accept whatever settlement Mrs. Dubose required. I expected labor: raking, replanting, weekends of penance among the ruined stems. Her sentence was stranger. Jem was to read to her every afternoon for a month, two hours at a time, in her dim room that smelled of medicine and old cloth. I went with him at first because the house frightened him more than he would admit, and because Atticus's silences had taught me that courage sometimes arrives disguised as accompaniment.

The room was close and shadowed. Mrs. Dubose lay propped in bed, a bell and an alarm clock within reach, her eyes bright with a malice that pain kept sharpening. Jem read from Ivanhoe in a voice that stumbled over the long sentences. At first she corrected him constantly, mocking his pronunciation, questioning his schooling, finding fault until the pages shook in his hands. Then, near the end of each session, she would grow distant. Her mouth worked. Sweat stood on her face. The alarm would ring like a reprieve, and Jessie, the girl who cared for her, would send us away.

I dreaded those afternoons and was fascinated by them in spite of myself. The sessions lengthened by minutes that felt deliberate. Jem read until his voice was hoarse. Mrs. Dubose seemed less interested in the story than in enduring until the clock released her. Only after she died did Atticus tell us the truth she had hidden behind cruelty: she had been dependent on morphine for years, prescribed for pain, and she had determined to free herself from it before she died. Reading distracted her through the worst intervals. She had held on, day after day, until her body no longer belonged to the drug.

She left Jem a box containing a single white camellia, perfect as snow. He recoiled from it at first, furious that the old woman could reach beyond death to trouble him. Atticus made us look longer. He said he wanted us to see that courage is not always a man with a gun in his hand. Real courage is beginning something when you know you may lose, and seeing it through anyway. Mrs. Dubose had won, by her own definition, in private, without applause.

I did not like her in life and I will not pretend death made her gentle in memory. She was sharp, prejudiced in some of her talk, and capable of wounding children for sport when pain rode her. But Atticus refused to let one truth erase another. She could be wrong about people and still be brave about herself. That distinction was difficult for me. I preferred my lessons sorted into heroes and villains. Maycomb kept handing me human beings instead.

Jem kept the camellia until the petals browned. He never said he was proud of having read to her, but he stopped describing the afternoons as punishment. Something in him had shifted, the same shift that began when he found his pants folded on the Radley fence: the recognition that other people's interiors are larger than the faces they show the street. He was growing up by inches, and sometimes the inches hurt.

For a long time afterward, the sight of a camellia made Jem go quiet in a way that had nothing to do with embarrassment. Atticus never forced him to explain it, and I learned from that restraint as much as from the reading itself. Some experiences are not improved by being talked into shape too quickly. Jem had entered a stranger's sickroom expecting a villain and had found a battle he could neither join nor dismiss. He carried that knowledge the way he carried the flower: carefully, and not entirely sure yet whether it was a burden or a gift.

CHAPTER 8
First Purchase Church

When Calpurnia took us to her church one Sunday, Atticus was away in Montgomery and she decided we would not attend our own church unsupervised, where our behavior might become a neighborhood bulletin. She scrubbed us with unusual severity, inspected Jem's ears and my dress, and warned us that we were representing the Finch household. I resented the implication until I understood that she was preparing us to enter a world where she, not we, would be judged by how her charges appeared.

First Purchase African M.E. Church stood in the Quarters, built, its name said, from the first earnings of freed slaves. The yard was swept, the windows plain, the congregation dressed with a dignity that made our own church seem careless by comparison. Some faces welcomed Calpurnia warmly. One woman, Lula, challenged her for bringing white children there. Her words were a mirror held up to every exclusion I had taken for granted. Calpurnia answered in the calm voice she used for storms, and the moment passed without surrendering its lesson.

Inside, there were no hymnbooks enough for everyone. Instead, Zeebo, Calpurnia's son, lined out the hymns, singing a line for the congregation to repeat in a rolling harmony that filled the small building more completely than any organ. I listened, astonished, not only by the beauty but by the discovery that literacy, which I had treated as a personal ornament, could be a communal bridge when one voice carried the words for many.

Reverend Sykes preached plainly and collected money not only for the church but for Helen Robinson, whose husband Tom was in jail awaiting trial and who could not find work with the charge hanging over her family. The doors were closed until the collection reached what the congregation judged necessary. It was charity with obligation in it, a community taxing itself because the larger town would not. I put in my dime and felt, obscurely, that I had been allowed to witness how people bear one another when institutions decline the burden.

On the walk home I asked Calpurnia why she spoke one way at our house and another among her own people. She explained, without apology, that talking above the company could sound like showing off, and that language is partly manners. I was troubled by the doubleness until she asked whether I spoke the same way to Atticus as I did in the schoolyard. The answer settled the matter. Calpurnia had two worlds and moved in both with intelligence. It was Maycomb, not Calpurnia, that insisted one of those worlds must be smaller.

That summer Aunt Alexandra arrived with a suitcase, opinions, and a mission to provide feminine influence before I disgraced the family name beyond repair. She was Atticus's sister, mountainous in certainty, devoted to the Finch heritage, and convinced that children should be reminded of the breeding that obliged them to behave. She moved into our home as if history itself had taken a room. Calpurnia's authority did not vanish, but it acquired a rival climate.

Alexandra believed in families the way some people believe in weather: as forces that explain everything. She wanted me in dresses, at teas, among the proper girls, learning the choreography of Maycomb womanhood. Atticus, caught between sisterly concern and his own looser faith in us, told me to behave as I thought best and then endured the consequences of divided government. I felt the walls of expectation narrow. A lady, in Alexandra's vocabulary, was not simply a woman. She was a performance with ancestry for a script.

Yet even Alexandra's arrival taught me something I did not expect. Beneath her severity was a fierce loyalty to Atticus and a terror that the coming trial would harm him. She disapproved of his defense of Tom Robinson in the language of family reputation, but she also understood the cost he was accepting. When she spoke of what "the family" owed Maycomb, I heard the other side of Atticus's creed: he believed a man owed his conscience first, even when the bill came due at his own table.

The trial was no longer a rumor at the edge of adult conversation. It had entered our church collection, our aunt's suitcases, our supper arguments. Tom Robinson's name was spoken in the Quarters with prayer and in the town with a ugliness that made my stomach tighten. I did not yet know the facts. I knew the weather was changing, and that Atticus stood where the storm would arrive first.


On the porch that evening, Calpurnia served lemonade as if the day had been ordinary, and perhaps that was her final lesson. She did not ask us to admire her church or pity it. She expected us to remember that the people there had names, families, obligations, and a claim on justice as real as any spoken in the courthouse square. When the trial came, I would hear Tom Robinson discussed as a case. Calpurnia had already made sure I had seen him first as a husband, a father, and a member of a congregation that refused to abandon him.


CHAPTER 9
Before the Storm

Tom Robinson was a black man who lived near the dump with his wife Helen and their children, worked for Mr. Link Deas, and was known among those who employed him as steady, respectful, and strong in his left arm because his right had been ruined in a cotton gin when he was a boy. He was accused by Mayella Ewell, the eldest daughter of Robert E. Lee Ewell, of attacking her in the Ewell house. In Maycomb, an accusation of that kind from a white woman against a black man was not treated as a claim to be tested. It was treated by many as a conclusion wearing the costume of a charge.

Atticus did not seek the case in the way men seek advancement. Judge Taylor appointed him, and Atticus accepted the defense as a duty he could not honorably refuse. He knew what the town expected: a performance of defense sufficient for the record and insufficient to disturb the verdict already reached in kitchens and barbershops. He intended something else. He intended to defend Tom Robinson as if the evidence mattered, because to Atticus it did, and because a courtroom that will not listen to evidence is only a mob with better furniture.

At school the case followed me in taunts. Children repeated their parents with the cruelty of parrots. Cecil Jacobs announced that my father defended people whose name he made into an insult. I fought when I could no longer bear the words, then went home ashamed because Atticus had asked me to keep my head high and my fists down. He said this was not a time to fight, that the trial would test more than Tom's freedom; it would test whether our family could endure being disapproved of by people whose approval was not worth the price.

Cousin Francis, at Christmas, supplied the lesson in a purer form. At Finch's Landing, amid Alexandra's rituals and Uncle Jack's good humor, Francis called Atticus names for taking the case and repeated slurs about our household until my restraint gave way entirely. I bloodied his nose and split my knuckles, then learned that Uncle Jack's justice, though swift, had been applied without hearing my side. Atticus, when he learned the whole story, did not excuse my language or my fists. But he made Uncle Jack understand that children ask hard questions and deserve answers that do not insult their intelligence.

Atticus told me then, in confidence I treasured, that he expected to lose. He used no varnish. An appeal might come later, but a Maycomb jury was unlikely to acquit Tom on the word of a black man against Ewells. I asked why he would fight a battle he expected to lose. He said some things must be done because not doing them would make it impossible to live with himself. He spoke of conscience as if it were a neighbor who could not be lied to indefinitely.

As the trial approached, the town's talk sharpened. Men gathered in our yard one evening, not a mob yet but a warning wearing hats. Atticus stood on the porch and received them with courtesy that had steel in it. Jem and I listened from inside, frightened by the adult music of voices kept low. Sheriff Tate was among them; so were men who bought seed from the same stores and prayed in the same churches as people who would later claim the verdict had nothing to do with them.

The night before the trial, Atticus took a light, an extension cord, and a chair to the jail and sat reading outside Tom Robinson's cell. Jem sensed purpose in the preparations and led Dill and me into the dark after him. We found Atticus under the bare bulb, calm as a man in his own study, when cars arrived and men got out with the soft purpose of those who have agreed beforehand not to call themselves a mob.

Mr. Walter Cunningham was among them, a man who had eaten at our table and paid Atticus for legal work in produce because cash was scarce and honor was not. I did the only thing I knew to do: I spoke to him as a person. I reminded him of his son, of entailments, of the dinner in our house. I made the crowd particular. It is harder to do violence in a group when a child insists on your name. Mr. Cunningham bent down, told me he would give Walter my regards, and the men went away into the night they had come from.

Atticus's hand shook when he touched Jem's head. Later Mr. Underwood, the newspaper man, revealed from his window above with a shotgun that Atticus had not been as alone as he believed. The town contained more kinds of courage than the mob had counted on. But the rescue, if it was one, was only for a night. In the morning the courthouse would fill, the jury would take its seats, and Maycomb would ask its law to bless what its customs had already decided.

CHAPTER 10
The Trial Begins

The courthouse square on the day of the trial looked like a picnic that had forgotten its innocence. Families arrived with baskets, children roamed, men discussed the proceedings in the tone reserved for public entertainment, and the black community waited with a patience that had nothing festive in it. Inside, the courtroom filled by color and custom: whites below, black spectators in the balcony, as if justice required a geography lesson before it could begin.

Jem, Dill, and I were not supposed to be there. We slipped in with the crowd and found no seats until Reverend Sykes, from First Purchase, made room for us in the balcony. From above I could see Atticus at the defense table, Tom Robinson beside him in a clean shirt, the Ewell side of the room, Judge Taylor presiding with an appearance of sleep that fooled no lawyer in Maycomb. The judge's inattention was a style. His courtroom was not.

Mr. Gilmer, the prosecutor, called Sheriff Heck Tate first. Tate described being summoned by Bob Ewell on the evening in question and finding Mayella beaten, with bruises around her neck and on the right side of her face. He said no doctor had been called. That absence passed quietly, though later it seemed to me a bell ringing in an empty room: in a case built on injury, nobody had thought medical testimony necessary, because the accusation itself was expected to do the work of proof.

Atticus, cross-examining, drew out the detail of the bruises with a patience that made Jem lean forward. If Mayella's right side was injured, the blows had likely come from a left-handed man. Atticus did not announce the conclusion. He let it sit in the jurors' minds like a seed in dry ground. He asked whether Tate had noticed anything else, whether the room showed a struggle, whether anyone had sent for a doctor. The answers built a picture of haste and certainty rather than investigation.

Bob Ewell took the stand with the swagger of a man who believed the courtroom owed him importance. He lived behind the dump in a cabin whose yard was a museum of other people's discards, kept his children mostly out of school, and survived by relief checks and the town's willingness to look away. On the stand he described hearing Mayella scream, running to the window, and seeing Tom Robinson attack her. His language was coarse enough for Judge Taylor to warn him, and his certainty had the polish of a story told many times to listeners who wanted it simple.

Atticus asked Ewell to write his name on an envelope. The courtroom watched the hand move. Bob Ewell wrote with his left hand and, pleased with himself, declared that it proved he was as literate as the next man. It proved something else to those keeping count. Jem whispered that we had him. I was young enough to believe evidence arranged itself into rescue. Atticus's face did not change. He knew the difference between proving a fact and persuading twelve men to let a fact outrank their habits.

Mayella's injuries, her father's left hand, Tom's ruined right arm: the geometry was forming. But the prosecution's case did not depend on geometry. It depended on the jury accepting that a white girl's fear and shame must be answered by a black man's punishment, whether or not the story held together. Ewell left the stand convinced he had performed well. Perhaps he had, for the audience he cared about.

During a recess Jem explained the proceedings to Dill and me with the confidence of a boy who has discovered that law can be intelligible. He believed Atticus was winning because, in the world Jem wanted to inhabit, facts accumulated until error had nowhere left to stand. I wanted to believe him. From the balcony, the black spectators sat very still, and their stillness told a different story about what experience had taught them to expect from the room below.

When court resumed, Mayella Ewell came to the stand, scrubbed and frightened, carrying the loneliness of a girl who tended geraniums in a yard of junk as if beauty itself were an accusation against her circumstances. She was nineteen, old enough to be burdened with her father's household and young enough to break under the weight of being believed or disbelieved in public. I felt, against my loyalty to Tom, a pity for her that confused me. Atticus had warned us that a person can be both dangerous and deserving of pity. Mayella was about to prove it.


Before she was sworn in, I studied the jury: farmers, townsmen, men who had nodded to Atticus in the street and would nod to him again whatever happened. They were not villains in a play. That was the hardest part to understand later. They were neighbors, and the verdict would not require them to feel wicked. It would only require them to choose the story their world had prepared for them over the one the evidence was patiently telling.


CHAPTER 11
Mayella, Tom, and the Truth

Mayella Ewell testified as if every question were a hand raised against her. She told the court she had asked Tom Robinson to chop up a chiffarobe for a nickel, that he had come inside the fence, and that he had attacked her. The story came out in pieces, some rehearsed, some panicked, all of it shaped by the knowledge that her father was watching and the town was listening. When Mr. Gilmer pressed her, she answered. When Atticus rose, she stiffened as if courtesy itself were a trick.

Atticus treated her with a gentleness that she mistook, or was taught to mistake, for mockery. He called her "ma'am" and asked simple questions. Did she have friends? She seemed not to understand the word in relation to herself. Who cared for the children when her father went into the woods? She did. Did she save the nickels she earned? She had been saving to take the children for ice cream, seven nickels hoarded one by one in a house where money vanished into her father's thirst. The detail broke through the accusation and showed a life so narrow that a kind word from a passerby could become an event dangerous enough to require a lie.

Atticus asked whether her father had ever beaten her. She denied it with heat. He asked whether Tom had hit her, whether the injuries matched a man whose left arm was strong and right arm useless. He reminded the court, without raising his voice, that Tom Robinson could not have strangled her with both hands or struck the right side of her face with the force described. Mayella's answers turned to anger. She accused the gentlemen of the jury of cowardice if they would not convict. Then she refused to answer more, and the silence she left behind was louder than her testimony.

Tom Robinson took the stand with a dignity that made the room's arrangements look shabby. His right arm hung shorter than his left, the hand small and useless from the cotton gin accident of his boyhood. He swore to tell the truth and then did, in a voice that did not ask for pity. He had passed the Ewell place often on his way to work. Mayella had asked him more than once to help with small chores: chopping kindling, carrying water, tending things her brothers could have done. He had helped because he was asked and because refusing a white woman's request carried risks of its own.

On the day in question, he said, Mayella called him to fix a door. Inside, the house was strangely quiet; the children were gone, sent to town for ice cream with the nickels she had saved. The door was not broken. When he stepped down from a chair after reaching to test the hinges, Mayella grabbed him around the legs, then put her arms around his waist, then kissed him. She said her father had never kissed her and that what her father did to her did not count. Tom's testimony did not accuse in anger. It revealed, with terrible clarity, that Mayella had reached across the code that governed both their lives and then, when her father saw through the window, had chosen the lie that would let her survive her own shame.

Tom ran, he said, not because he had attacked her but because a black man found in that scene had no safe explanation available. Mr. Gilmer's cross-examination tried to turn decency into insolence. He asked why Tom helped without pay. Tom answered that he felt sorry for her. The phrase landed in the courtroom like a stone through glass. A black man's pity for a white woman offended the hierarchy more deeply than the alleged crime had, because it implied that he could see her life clearly and judge it lacking. Dill, beside me, began to cry and had to be taken out by Jem. He said it made him sick the way Mr. Gilmer talked to Tom. It was the prosecutor's tone, Dill said, as if Tom were not a man at all.

Atticus's closing was quiet. He asked the jury to set aside the assumption that all black men lie, cheat, and cannot be trusted around women; he named it as the evil assumption it was. He said Mayella had been motivated by guilt and fear after breaking a rigid code, and that she had accused Tom to remove the evidence of her own offense. He reminded them that Tom's testimony had not changed, that the medical evidence did not exist, that the injuries pointed away from the accused, and that a courtroom is the one place where men are supposed to be equal even when the society outside is not.

He did not beg. He asked for a duty to be performed. "In the name of God," he said in effect, "do your duty." The exact words matter less in my memory than the stillness that followed, the feeling that Atticus had placed the whole case where it belonged: not in the town's gossip, not in the Ewell yard, but in the consciences of twelve men who had sworn an oath and would now discover what the oath was worth.

CHAPTER 12
The Verdict and After

The jury stayed out longer than Maycomb expected. Hours passed. Evening settled on the courtroom, and still the twelve men did not return. Jem took the delay as hope. In his new faith in evidence, a long deliberation meant argument, and argument meant at least one juror had been moved. Reverend Sykes, who knew the customs of such rooms better than we did, warned him gently not to get our hopes up. He had never seen a jury decide in favor of a colored man over a white man, he said, not in a case like this.

I fell asleep against Jem and woke to the peculiar quiet that enters a room before bad news. The jury filed in without looking at Tom Robinson. I had been told as a child that a jury never looks at a man it has convicted, and whether the saying is always true or only true that night, I have never forgotten the averted faces. The foreman handed the verdict to Judge Taylor. Guilty. The word moved through the courtroom with the finality of a door closing in a house where someone has died.

Tom stood to hear it with a composure I can still see. Atticus gathered his papers, spoke to him briefly, touched his shoulder, and walked down the center aisle alone. As he passed beneath the balcony, the black spectators rose to their feet. Reverend Sykes told me to stand because my father was passing. I stood among people who had no reason to honor the system that had failed them and every reason to honor the man who had tried to make it honest. It remains one of the most solemn moments of my childhood.

Jem cried on the way home with the anger of someone whose map of the world has been torn. He kept saying it was not right, as if repetition might repair the fact. Atticus was tired in a way sleep does not cure. He told us an appeal would be filed and that Tom would go to Enfield Prison Farm while it was prepared. He also told us, because he would not lie to make the night easier, that the verdict had been likely from the beginning. Jem asked how a jury could convict on such evidence. Atticus answered that they had done it before, that day, and would do it again, and that when they did, it seemed only children wept over it.

In the days that followed, Maycomb returned to its routines with an ease that felt obscene. Some people praised Atticus for a defense they had never wanted him to win. Others treated the verdict as proof that order had survived. Food appeared on our back steps from the black community: chicken, bread, pickled pigs' feet, gifts of gratitude laid quietly where Calpurnia would find them. Atticus's eyes filled when he saw them. He told Calpurnia to thank everyone and to say it was too much; people who had almost nothing had given as if they were rich.

Aunt Alexandra worried over Jem, over Atticus, over the family name, and surprised me by showing a tenderness beneath her discipline. Miss Maudie, who had watched the trial from her own hard clarity, told Jem that there were people in Maycomb who had been born with a sense of justice and that Atticus was not as alone as the verdict made him seem. Judge Taylor had appointed him deliberately. Mr. Underwood had covered the jail with a gun. A Cunningham on the jury had argued for acquittal longer than anyone expected. Progress in Maycomb, she suggested, moved in inches paid for by people willing to be disliked.

Then came the news that ended argument. Tom Robinson, despairing of appeal or unable to bear the cage after innocence had failed to free him, tried to climb the fence at the prison yard in broad daylight. The guards said they shouted for him to stop. He was shot many times, seventeen bullet holes counted by people who repeated the number with disbelief or indifference depending on the porch. Atticus and Calpurnia went together to tell Helen Robinson, who collapsed before the words were fully spoken, as if her body had understood before her mind could afford to.

Mr. Underwood wrote in his newspaper that it was a sin to kill cripples, whatever the circumstances, and compared Tom's death to the senseless slaughter of songbirds by hunters and children. The phrase stayed with me. Tom had harmed no one. He had helped because he pitied a lonely girl, and the town had answered his pity first with a cell and then with bullets. If a mockingbird's crime is innocence, Maycomb had found a man to punish for it.

Bob Ewell, whose lie had won, was not satisfied. Victory had not made him important in the way he craved. He spat in Atticus's face in the street and threatened him. Atticus wiped his face, declined to fight, and told us Ewell needed to vent his anger on someone because we had made him look foolish in court. It seemed generous to the point of danger. Jem thought so too. A man who has been publicly shamed and privately rotten does not always stop at spitting. Autumn came with school pageants and lengthening dark, and under the ordinary sounds of the town I began to hear the scrape of a threat looking for a smaller target than Atticus Finch.


CHAPTER 13
The Night of the Pageant

Bob Ewell got a job after the trial and lost it almost as quickly, blaming Atticus in public for a failure that belonged to his own laziness. He followed Helen Robinson on her way to work, muttering filth from the roadside until Mr. Link Deas, who employed her, threatened him with the law and made the threat plain enough to work for a while. Judge Taylor heard someone prowling around his house one night and thereafter kept a gun within reach of his reading chair. These incidents traveled the town as gossip. In our house they arrived as a pattern Jem and I were old enough to notice and too young to solve.

Atticus refused to be alarmed in the manner Ewell wanted. He said the man had had his chance to fight at the post office corner and had chosen spit instead of fists. He believed Ewell's venom was spent. It was one of the few times I have known Atticus to misread a human being, and he misread him because he judged by a code Ewell did not share. Atticus thought shame eventually exhausts itself. In Bob Ewell, shame fermented.

On Halloween the school held a pageant celebrating Maycomb County's agricultural glories. Children were assigned the produce of the region and processed across the stage as advertisements with legs. I was a ham. The costume, built of chicken wire and cloth, covered me from shoulders to knees, restricted my arms, narrowed my vision to a slit, and made me dependent on Jem to guide me. I considered it undignified. Jem, who was too old for such performances, considered escorting a ham to the school auditorium a burden he would mention for years.

Aunt Alexandra did not come with us. She was tired, she said, and Atticus was away on business connected with the legislature of his responsibilities. The night was unusually dark, the kind of darkness in which the familiar road to the school seemed longer because the usual landmarks had withdrawn. Jem carried my costume money and a flashlight we did not yet need. We passed the Radley place with the old jokes ready on our tongues, and Cecil Jacobs leaped out to frighten us, proving that some terrors still announce themselves with laughter.

At the school I waited in my ham shell while Mrs. Merriweather's pageant unfolded with poetry, music, and agricultural pride. I missed my cue in a drowse and entered late to laughter that followed me off the stage. Mortified, I refused to go home until the crowd had thinned. Jem, to his credit, did not hurry me. He understood humiliation well enough by then to let it cool before asking me to walk inside it.

The walk home began in high wind and darkness so complete that Jem kept one hand on my costume to steer me. The auditorium lights fell behind. Somewhere near the big oak by the Radley property, Jem stopped. He had heard something: not the ordinary night sounds, but a repeated swishing, as if someone were following in the leaves and pausing when we paused. I laughed at first because fear sometimes borrows laughter's coat. Then I heard it too.

We quickened. The sounds quickened. Jem shouted for me to run, but the ham costume betrayed me; I stumbled, the wire frame twisted, and the world became noise, impact, and the smell of earth. Someone seized me and crushed the costume until breathing hurt. Jem fought with a courage that still makes my throat close to remember. I heard him cry out, a sound cut short by a crack I did not understand until later. Then there was a struggle I could not see, bodies in the dark, a man's breathing, and suddenly the pressure on me was gone.

When I freed myself enough to stand, the road was a confusion of shapes. Jem lay still. A man I did not know was carrying him toward our house with a tenderness that contradicted the violence of the minutes before. I followed in the ruined costume, barefoot on the cold ground, calling Jem's name. Atticus and Alexandra met us in an eruption of light and questions. Dr. Reynolds came. Sheriff Tate came. Jem's arm had been broken badly at the elbow. Bob Ewell lay dead under the tree with a kitchen knife beneath his ribs.

The facts assembled slowly, and some refused to assemble at all. Mr. Tate questioned me in my bedroom while the doctor worked on Jem. I told what I could: the following footsteps, the attack, Jem's scream, the stranger who intervened. In the corner of the room stood a man I had never seen up close, pale as the wall behind him, his hands nervous, his eyes wet with the strain of being among people. When Atticus spoke to him gently and called him Arthur, I knew him by the gifts, by the blanket, by the mended pants. Boo Radley had come out of the house at last, not to frighten children, but to save them.

CHAPTER 14
Standing on the Radley Porch

Jem slept under the doctor's medicine while the adults decided what the night would be allowed to mean. In the living room, Sheriff Tate and Atticus argued in the low voices of men who respect each other and disagree about mercy. Tate insisted that Bob Ewell had fallen on his own knife in the dark. Atticus, believing at first that Jem had killed Ewell in the struggle, wanted no cover-up for his own son; he said Jem should not begin life with a rumor and a lie for a shield. It took Tate's patience to make the truth plain enough for even Atticus's honesty to accept.

The sheriff knew, or had reason enough to know, that Arthur Radley had driven the knife while defending us. He also knew what a trial, or even public gratitude, would do to a man who had lived his whole life behind a closed door. To drag Boo into the limelight, Tate said, with his shy nature, would be a sin. The word landed in the room and found its old companions: a mockingbird, a crippled man shot on a prison fence, a neighbor whose only crimes had been committed against him by other people's imaginations.

Atticus turned to me and asked whether I understood. I did, in the way children sometimes understand the essential thing before they can explain the machinery around it. Boo had given us gifts, mended our clothes, warmed us in a fire, and finally given us our lives. To repay him by making him a spectacle would be like killing a mockingbird. Atticus put his arm around me and said that most people are kind, when you finally see them. It was not naïveté. It was a conclusion bought at a price I hope never to pay in full.

Arthur asked, in a voice so soft I had to lean toward the sound of it, whether I would walk him home. He had never asked me for anything. I took his hand as if he were a child returning from a party, and we went out into the night he knew better than I did. The street looked ordinary, which seemed impossible after all that had happened on it. From the Radley porch I saw our neighborhood arranged as Arthur must have seen it for years: Miss Maudie's flowers, Miss Stephanie's windows, the places where children played and quarreled and grew.

Standing there, I did the thing Atticus had been teaching me since Miss Caroline's classroom. I climbed, in my mind, into Arthur Radley's skin and looked out at us. I saw Jem and Scout and Dill as he had seen us: small, loud, frightened, funny, cruel in our ignorance and dear in our possibilities. I saw why the gifts had been chosen with such care and why the giver could not stay to be thanked. Some people love the world best by tending it from the edge of sight. It does not make their love smaller. It makes the rest of us responsible for not demanding that it perform.

I walked home alone because that was what he wanted. Atticus was waiting in Jem's room, reading The Gray Ghost in the chair beside the bed. Jem's broken arm would heal shorter in appearance than the other, but he would mind it less than the memory of that night if we gave him time. I climbed into Atticus's lap and told him the story he was reading was not really scary, and that most things are not nearly as frightening once you know the people inside them. He carried me toward bed with the patience of a man who had carried heavier weights through darker rooms.

Years have passed, and Maycomb has changed in the slow ways towns change and resisted change in the slower ways towns specialize in. I cannot claim the trial redeemed us. Tom Robinson was not restored by our sorrow, and Helen's children grew up with a grave where a father should have been. But I can say that two children learned to look twice at every closed door, every verdict, every easy story told by people who prefer their judgments ready-made.

Atticus taught us that conscience is not a garment put on for public occasions. It is the skin beneath. Jem learned that courage can read aloud to a dying woman, argue in a jury room without winning, or fight in the dark for a sister trapped in a foolish costume. I learned that the neighbor we made into a ghost had been practicing neighborliness all along, in whispers of gifts and folds of cloth, until the night his kindness had to step into the open and bleed for its place in the story.

When I think of Maycomb now, I do not think first of the courthouse or the verdict, though they are there. I think of a porch at the end of a street, and of standing on it long enough to see the world from inside another life. That is where the story ends for me: not with a monster exposed, but with a man allowed to go home in peace, and a girl walking back through the dark with her father's lamp ahead of her, less afraid of what she could not yet understand.
