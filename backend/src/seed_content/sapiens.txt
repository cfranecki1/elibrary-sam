Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind
Yuval Noah Harari

CHAPTER 1
An Animal of No Significance

About 13.8 billion years ago, matter, energy, time and space came into being in the event we call the Big Bang. About 3.8 billion years ago, on a planet called Earth, certain molecules combined into the first living organisms. And about 70,000 years ago, organisms belonging to one particular species, Homo sapiens, began to form structures more elaborate than anything the planet had seen: cultures. This book is the story of how that one species of ape came to rule the world, and it begins with a humbling fact. For most of our history, we were of no significance at all.

Humans did not begin with us. The genus Homo emerged in Africa roughly 2.5 million years ago, and over the following millennia several distinct human species walked the earth at the same time. Homo erectus spread across Asia and survived for nearly two million years, longer than we have so far managed. Homo neanderthalensis, stocky and strong, thrived in the cold of Europe and western Asia. The Denisovans ranged across Siberia and East Asia, while tiny Homo floresiensis, barely a metre tall, lived on the Indonesian island of Flores. There were others besides. The idea that one human species alone inhabits the earth is a recent accident of history, not a law of nature.

Our own species, Homo sapiens, evolved in East Africa around 300,000 years ago, and for the first two-thirds of our existence we were thoroughly unremarkable. If a visitor had surveyed the savannah 100,000 years ago, nothing about us would have stood out. We were mid-sized omnivores, neither the fastest nor the strongest animals around, preyed upon by lions and hyenas, and ranked somewhere in the middle of the food chain. We gathered plants, hunted small game, cracked open bones left by mightier predators to suck out the marrow, and tried not to be eaten.

We did possess two unusual traits, though both came at a price. The first was walking upright, which freed our hands for tools and let us scan the horizon, but narrowed the pelvis and made childbirth dangerous and painful. Human babies are born helpless and stay dependent for years, which forced our ancestors into tight cooperative groups simply to raise children. The second trait was an exceptionally large brain. Brains are expensive organs: ours consume about a quarter of the body's energy at rest, and in most mammals the figure is under a tenth. For hundreds of thousands of years we paid that cost without any obvious return. A big brain alone did not make us rulers of anything.

Tools were part of the compensation. Humans had been shaping stone for more than two million years before sapiens appeared, and by 300,000 years ago our ancestors hafted spear points, worked wood and bone, and planned hunts days in advance. Meat and marrow from large carcasses, obtained by hunting or by driving predators from their kills, supplied the dense calories a big brain demands. None of this was unique to sapiens, however. Neanderthals were superb hunters of mammoth and rhino, buried their dead, cared for injured companions who could no longer hunt, and may have made art. Any honest account must admit that if a Neanderthal and a sapiens had traded places in 100,000 BC, no observer could have predicted which lineage would inherit the earth.

One genuine breakthrough did arrive before our rise: the taming of fire. Some human species were using fire regularly by about 300,000 years ago, possibly much earlier. Fire gave warmth, light and protection from predators at night. More importantly, it let humans cook. Cooking softens food, kills parasites and germs, and makes nutrients far easier to absorb. Chimpanzees spend around five hours a day chewing raw food; humans need less than one hour of eating cooked food to obtain the same energy. Shorter guts and smaller teeth followed, and the energy saved may have helped feed our growing brains. Fire also gave a puny ape a weapon: a band of humans with torches could burn a forest or drive off a pride of lions.

Still, as late as 70,000 years ago, sapiens remained marginal creatures. Then, in a geological blink, everything changed. Sapiens spread out of Africa, reached Australia by about 45,000 years ago, crossed into the Americas, and everywhere they arrived, the other human species vanished. The Neanderthals were gone by roughly 40,000 years ago; the Denisovans and the little people of Flores disappeared around the same era. Scholars still debate whether our ancestors outcompeted them, interbred with them, or wiped them out. DNA evidence shows some mixing occurred, since many people today carry small amounts of Neanderthal or Denisovan ancestry. But the outcome is not in doubt: within a few tens of thousands of years, a single human species remained, and it was us.

Why did sapiens, of all the human species, inherit the earth? Our close relatives had big brains, tools and fire too. Something happened inside the sapiens mind, a mutation in the way we think and communicate, that gave us an advantage no other animal has ever possessed. To understand our conquest of the world, we have to understand that revolution in cognition, because it is the foundation of everything that followed: myths, money, empires and nations all rest upon it.

CHAPTER 2
The Cognitive Revolution

Between about 70,000 and 30,000 years ago, Homo sapiens began doing things no animal had done before. They built boats and crossed open sea to Australia. They carved figurines, such as the lion-man of ivory found in a German cave, depicting a creature that does not exist in nature. They traded over long distances, sewed clothes with needles, painted cave walls with haunting skill, and buried their dead with grave goods. Genetic evidence suggests the cause was a chance mutation in the internal wiring of the sapiens brain, sometimes called the Tree of Knowledge mutation. Whatever its biological basis, its consequence was a new kind of language, and language changed everything.

Animal communication systems can be surprisingly rich. A monkey can warn its troop that a lion is coming, and some species distinguish between different predators. But all such systems share a hard limit: they can only describe what exists. A monkey can say "Careful! A lion!" but never "The lion we saw by the river yesterday is the guardian spirit of our troop." Sapiens language is astonishingly flexible. With a finite set of sounds we can produce an infinite number of sentences, and crucially, we can talk about things that do not exist at all: spirits, nations, companies, rights, gods and money. This capacity for fiction is not a curious side effect of language. It is the master key to human history.

Language evolved, in large part, for gossip. The anthropologist Robin Dunbar observed that social grooming, picking through each other's fur, is how other primates maintain alliances, but grooming is one-to-one and slow. It caps group size at roughly fifty individuals for chimps. Humans faced the same problem of trust and alliance, but gossip, talking about third parties, let one person maintain relationships with many others at once. Dunbar's research suggests the natural limit for a human group bound by personal knowledge is around 150 people, a figure that still appears in military companies, villages and businesses today. Gossip also polices reputations: cheats and free-riders get talked about, which keeps cooperation honest without any police force.

Gossip was only one of language's gifts. Language also let humans pool information about the physical world on an unprecedented scale. A band's knowledge of which mushrooms kill, where water hides in a dry season, how bison behave when the wind shifts, could be spoken, remembered, corrected and passed to children who had never seen a drought or a hunt. No individual needed to learn everything the hard way, because the group carried a library in its conversations. Knowledge became cumulative: each generation began where the last left off, rather than starting again from instinct. This ratchet effect, slow at first, is what eventually separates human technology from the fixed toolkits of other apes, whose cleverest innovations die with their inventors because they cannot be described precisely enough to spread. Even the social world became information: who owed whom a favour, who could be trusted with a secret, whose hunting stories were exaggerated. Reputation, tracked in words, became a kind of currency long before coins existed.

But even gossip has a ceiling. Bands larger than about 150 cannot hold together on personal knowledge alone, and ants and bees, which cooperate in the tens of thousands, do so through rigid instinct rather than flexible choice. Bees cannot renegotiate their monarchy; chimpanzees can renegotiate alliances, but only within a small troop. Sapiens alone broke the barrier, and they broke it with stories. Because sapiens can believe collectively in things that exist only in the imagination, complete strangers can cooperate in vast numbers, and can change the rules of cooperation overnight simply by changing the story they tell.

Consider what this means in practice. Two Catholics who have never met can set out together on a crusade or pool money to build a hospital, because both believe in God, the crucifixion and heaven. Two Serbs who have never met will risk their lives for one another because both believe in the Serbian nation, its homeland and its flag. Two employees of a car company can collaborate on a design although neither knows the other, because both believe in the company that employs them. None of these entities, God, nation or company, can be touched, weighed or measured. They exist because millions of people tell the same story and act as if it were true.

This is the point on which the rest of this book turns. Sapiens rule the world not because we are the strongest or cleverest animals in any simple sense, but because we are the only animal that can cooperate flexibly in very large numbers. And we do it by believing shared fictions. The chapters that follow trace how those fictions, and the real technologies and crops that accompanied them, transformed an insignificant ape into the master, and increasingly the maker, of life on earth.

CHAPTER 3
The Power of Shared Myths

In 1896 a French lawyer's son named Armand Peugeot founded a company to manufacture cars. That company still exists today, employing hundreds of thousands of people, producing millions of vehicles, and owning factories, bank accounts and legal rights. But what, exactly, is Peugeot? It is not its factories, which could burn down while the company survives. It is not its workers or managers, all of whom could be replaced. It is not the lion emblem stamped on its cars. Peugeot is a legal fiction, a story invented by lawyers and believed by everyone: banks lend to it, courts protect it, customers trust it. Kill every employee and demolish every factory, and Peugeot could still borrow money and start again. The company belongs to a class of things that exist only because our collective imagination says they do.

Historians call such things intersubjective realities. A stone is objective: it exists whether or not anyone believes in it. My personal belief that the stone is a god is subjective: it lives in one mind and dies with it. But when millions of people share a belief, that belief becomes a force in the world as solid as any stone. Money has value because everyone believes it does. Borders stop armies because everyone treats them as real. Laws imprison people because judges, police and citizens all accept the story. The intersubjective web of stories is the operating system of human civilisation.

Shared myths do more than enable cooperation; they also define its terms, including its cruelties. The Code of Hammurabi, inscribed in Babylon around 1776 BC, divided society into superiors, commoners and slaves, and priced their lives differently: the eye of a superior was worth more than the life of a commoner. The American Declaration of Independence of 1776 announced the opposite myth, that all men are created equal and endowed with inalienable rights. Neither claim is a biological fact. Evolution knows no equality and no rights; organisms have only survival and reproduction. Both documents are stories, but they are stories that built real societies, one of hierarchy and one of democracy, because millions organised their behaviour around them.

Myths are not maintained by belief alone. Every durable fiction is dressed in physical clothing: flags and anthems, crowns and courtrooms, uniforms, rituals, monuments and schoolbooks. A judge's robe and a marble courthouse exist to make an invisible thing, the law, feel solid. A graduation ceremony, a wedding ring, a national holiday: all are technologies for refreshing a story before it fades. This is why revolutions attack symbols so ferociously, toppling statues and renaming streets. They understand, instinctively, that the story lives in its symbols, and that to kill the fiction you must first make its clothing ridiculous.

Myths are also astonishingly changeable. Because a social order rests on belief rather than on iron laws of nature, sapiens can transform it within a generation by telling a new story. The French Revolution is the classic case: within a few years, the myth of the divine right of kings, believed for centuries, collapsed, and the myth of the sovereignty of the people took its place. Priests, prophets, lawyers and politicians are the professional storytellers who maintain these fictions, adjusting them when they crack. Even science, our most reliable knowledge system, cooperates with myths: research is funded because societies believe in progress, and its findings are woven into the stories nations tell about themselves.

Yet there is a trap built into every shared myth. Once a story organises a society, it creates institutions, habits and interests that make the story very hard to change, even when it harms the people inside it. Hierarchies invented thousands of years ago, based on imagined differences of caste, race or gender, persisted long after anyone could justify them, because each generation was educated into the story from birth and the whole machinery of law and custom enforced it. Discrimination is rarely a conspiracy of the powerful; more often it is a vicious circle in which an accidental historical event hardens into a myth, and the myth then reproduces the conditions that seem to prove it true.

None of this means myths are lies to be discarded. Without them, no society larger than a village could exist at all. The challenge, which will recur throughout this book, is that we forget our stories are stories. We treat nations, money, corporations and rights as features of the universe rather than as tools we invented, and we then become their servants. The first step to understanding humankind is to see the fictions clearly. The second is to see what those fictions did to the animals, and the humans, who lived inside them, beginning with the most successful revolution our species ever launched against its own way of life: farming.

CHAPTER 4
A Day in the Life of Foragers

For roughly 95 per cent of our species' existence, all humans lived the same way: in small bands of foragers who hunted animals and gathered wild plants. To understand what we later gained and lost, it helps to spend an imagined day in such a band 30,000 years ago. At dawn the camp stirs. A group of women and children heads out with digging sticks and baskets, knowing from years of accumulated knowledge exactly which roots are ripe in this valley, which berries are poisonous, and where the bees nested last season. A party of men follows the tracks of deer, reading bent grass and dung with a precision modern trackers still admire. An old woman who can no longer walk far minds the fire and the toddlers, and her memory of the drought fifty years ago, and which waterholes survived it, may one day save the whole band.

The forager diet was remarkably varied. A single band might eat dozens of species of plants, along with game, fish, insects, eggs and honey. This diversity delivered a balanced mix of proteins, vitamins and minerals, and made famine less likely: if one food failed, others remained. Foragers also suffered far fewer epidemics than later farmers, because they lived in small, mobile groups without domestic animals to share diseases with. Skeletal evidence suggests ancient foragers were generally taller and healthier than the farmers who came after them, and they reached old age more often than we assume; a forager who survived childhood had a fair chance of living into their sixties or seventies.

Knowledge was the band's true wealth, and it was stored in people. With no writing, everything the group knew, hundreds of plants and their seasons, animal behaviour, water sources, genealogies, the routes of migration and the stories that explained them, lived in memory, above all in the memory of the old. Grandmothers were libraries. Children learned not in lessons but in life: playing at hunting with miniature bows, tagging along on gathering trips, listening at night to stories in which practical knowledge and moral instruction were woven together. Education was total and continuous, and it produced adults whose competence in their environment exceeded anything a modern person achieves in theirs. A forager dropped alone in a city would be helpless; but so would a city dweller dropped alone in the Kalahari, and only one of those environments is the one our minds were built for.

Nor did they work themselves to exhaustion. Anthropologists who studied surviving forager peoples, such as the San of the Kalahari, found that obtaining food took roughly 35 to 45 hours a week, less than a modern office worker spends at a desk, and the work itself was varied and social rather than repetitive. The anthropologist Marshall Sahlins provocatively called foragers "the original affluent society": affluent not because they had much, but because they wanted little, and could meet those wants with ease. With no fields to guard and no stores to defend, foragers carried almost everything they owned. Possessions were a burden to people who moved camp regularly, and generosity, not accumulation, was the route to status.

The forager world was not paradise, and honesty requires saying so. Violence between and within bands was common, and skeletons often bear the marks of it. Infanticide was practised when a band could not feed another mouth, and the very old or sick might be abandoned when the group moved on. Child mortality was high by modern standards. But the inner life of foragers was probably richer than the stereotype of brutish cave-dwellers suggests. Their intimate knowledge of hundreds of species, seasons, stars and stories made their mental world dense and demanding. Animist beliefs, in which humans share a world of spirits with animals, plants and places, encouraged a sense of kinship with nature rather than dominion over it.

One member of the camp deserves special mention: the dog. Dogs were domesticated from wolves at least 15,000 years ago, long before any farm animal or crop, probably because wolves that tolerated human camps gained scraps, and humans gained alarms, hunting partners and warmth. The bond was emotional as well as practical; a puppy buried in a human grave 14,000 years ago in Germany testifies that the relationship was already deep. The dog was the first chapter of a much larger story, the domestication of plants and animals, which would soon remake the human world entirely. It is a measure of the bond that when foragers finally settled into villages, they brought the dog with them into the farming world, where it remains to this day, the one domesticate that was never primarily a source of food. For the foragers themselves, though, life had been stable for tens of thousands of years. Nothing in their experience suggested that within a few millennia, almost every human on earth would be a farmer, bent over wheat fields, and that the forager way of life would survive only at the planet's margins.

CHAPTER 5
The Agricultural Revolution: History's Biggest Fraud

Around 9500 BC, people in the Levant began deliberately planting wheat and tending goats. Over the following millennia, farming was invented independently in several places: wheat and barley in the Middle East, rice and millet in China, maize, beans and squash in Central America, potatoes in the Andes, and more. By about 3500 BC the revolution was essentially complete across much of the world, and it has never been reversed. On the surface, the logic seemed irresistible: why wander in search of food when you can grow it beside your home? Yet when we examine the evidence of individual lives, a startling verdict emerges. The Agricultural Revolution kept more humans alive under worse conditions. It was, in a memorable phrase, history's biggest fraud.

Consider the daily reality. A forager obtained varied food with moderate effort. A farmer's life was relentless labour: clearing stones, ploughing, sowing, weeding, guarding crops from animals, hauling water, harvesting, threshing, grinding. The work began at dawn, it never ended, and much of it fell to children. The farmer's diet, meanwhile, was worse. Foragers ate dozens of species; farmers depended on a handful of starchy staples, rich in calories but poor in vitamins and minerals. A bad harvest of the single main crop meant starvation. Crowded villages beside penned animals became factories of disease: measles, smallpox, tuberculosis and flu all jumped to humans from domesticated animals or thrived in dense settlements. Skeletons of early farmers show shorter stature, rotten teeth, anaemia and bones deformed by labour.

Why, then, did anyone farm? The answer is that the revolution was not a decision but a slide. Each step seemed small and sensible. A band that planted a little wheat near a good camp ate a bit better that year. A slightly larger population made returning to foraging harder, because the wild foods of the area could not feed so many mouths. A good harvest produced more surviving children, who needed more fields, which produced more children. Luxuries became necessities: once your grandparents built a permanent house and stored grain, abandoning them meant losing everything. Within a few generations the trap had closed. Farming did not spread because it made individuals happier; it spread because farming communities produced far more people than foraging ones, and sheer numbers won.

Curiously, the first steps toward farming may not have been about food at all. At Göbekli Tepe in modern Turkey, hunter-gatherers around 9500 BC carved and raised enormous stone pillars, decorated with animals, in what appears to be a ceremonial complex, centuries before any evidence of farming nearby. Feeding the builders of such monuments would have required organised, large-scale food production, and some archaeologists suspect the causality ran backwards: the demands of ritual, feasting and monument-building pushed foragers to cultivate wild grains intensively, and farming followed the temple rather than the other way round. Whether or not that specific theory holds, the site proves that foragers were capable of vast cooperative projects before agriculture, and that the revolution's roots lie as much in belief and social organisation as in climate or hunger.

Population genetics sealed the outcome. A fertile farming valley might support a hundred people per square mile where a foraging territory supported a handful. Farming peoples expanded, absorbed or pushed aside foragers, and carried their languages and genes with them. From the perspective of evolution, which measures success purely by copies of DNA, the revolution was a triumph: around 10,000 years ago there were perhaps five to eight million humans; by AD 1800 there were nearly a billion. But evolution's scoreboard is not the individual's. The typical farmer worked harder, ate worse, sickened more often and died younger than the typical forager. More human lives, of lower quality: that was the bargain.

Nor did the surplus of farming flow mainly to the labourers. Grain, unlike wild food, can be stored, measured and taxed, and stored food can be stolen. Farming therefore created something new in the world: an elite of kings, priests, soldiers and officials who lived off the surplus extracted from peasants, and who used the shared myths of gods and kingship to make that extraction seem natural and just. The peasant's extra produce fed the pyramid builders, the armies and the scribes. Civilisation, with its cities and wonders, was built on the farmer's bent back, and the farmer rarely tasted its fruits.

The lesson generalises beyond agriculture. Again and again in history, humans have adopted changes that promised comfort and delivered toil, because each step was easy and the way back kept narrowing. The Agricultural Revolution is the grandest example of the pattern, and its central character is not the farmer at all. It is a grass. To see the revolution from the grass's point of view is to realise that perhaps we did not domesticate wheat. Perhaps wheat domesticated us.

CHAPTER 6
How Wheat Domesticated Humans

Ten thousand years ago, wheat was an unremarkable wild grass growing in a limited band of the Middle East. Today it covers millions of square kilometres of the planet's surface, from the plains of North America to the steppes of Russia and the fields of Australia. By the only measure evolution recognises, copies made of itself, wheat is one of the most successful plants in the history of life. How did a humble grass achieve this? It did so by manipulating an ape into doing all its work. From wheat's perspective, the bargain with Homo sapiens was superb, and it is worth listing what the ape was required to provide.

Wheat is a demanding plant. It does not like rocky ground, so sapiens broke their backs clearing stones from fields, and their descendants still do. It needs abundant water, so humans dug irrigation channels, dug wells, and hauled water in buckets until their joints wore out. It is defenceless against insects, rabbits and competing weeds, so humans stood guard, weeded on their knees under the sun, and fought off locusts and blight. It needs nutrients, so humans burned forests for ash, and later spread the dung of animals across the fields, recycling the fertiliser of other domesticates back into the soil. In exchange, wheat offered calories. Nothing more.

The human body paid the invoice. Skeletons from the first farming villages show the damage clearly: slipped discs, arthritic knees and toes, hernias, and skulls marked by the loads women carried grinding grain for hours each day. Nor could the farmer wander. Wheat tied people to permanent houses beside their fields, and permanence brought new dangers: stored grain attracted thieves and tax collectors, walls had to be built, wars had to be fought over land and water. The forager's world had been dangerous in moments; the farmer's world was dangerous as a condition. Anxiety became a permanent feature of life, worry about rain, pests, rivers, raiders and rulers, a psychological burden our species still carries.

Farming also invented a new relationship to things: property. A forager owns what she can carry and defends little else. A farmer owns land, and land can be inherited, disputed, divided and stolen. With property came inheritance, and with inheritance came an obsession with legitimate heirs, patrolling the sexuality of women in a way forager bands rarely did, and the patriarchal family structures that still echo today. Land records, boundary stones and inheritance law all descend from this moment. So does a new kind of war: not raids for honour or captives, but systematic conquest of territory, because territory was now wealth itself. The farmer could not walk away from a conflict, as a forager could. His wealth was rooted in the ground, and so he had to stand and fight for it, or lose everything his family had built.

Even the winners of the revolution, measured by DNA, included creatures whose individual lives became miserable. The chicken, a jungle bird of Southeast Asia, is now the most widespread bird on earth, and by numbers one of the most successful animals ever. Yet the overwhelming majority of chickens live short lives of confinement and slaughter. The same is true of cattle, pigs and sheep: evolutionarily triumphant, individually wretched. This gap between evolutionary success and individual suffering is one of the deepest lessons of the Agricultural Revolution. A species can conquer the world and make nearly every one of its members miserable in the process. Wheat, chickens, cattle and, on the evidence of the bones, Homo sapiens all did exactly that.

The pattern did not end with wheat. Rice demanded flooded terraces and bound its farmers to even more gruelling labour in the paddies of Asia. Maize in the Americas required careful breeding from a near-inedible ancestor and chained its growers to endless processing to make it safe and nutritious. In each case the plant spread, the human population grew, and the humans worked harder for a narrower diet. We flatter ourselves that domestication was our invention and our tool. It may be truer to say that a handful of plants exploited our talents, our muscles, our water engineering and our willingness to suffer for a full stomach, and rode us to world dominion.

What farming unquestionably did produce, beyond calories, was surplus, and surplus changed the scale of human society. Grain could be counted, stored in warehouses, transported and traded. It could feed people who did not farm: full-time potters, priests, soldiers and kings. The villages of farmers became towns, the towns became cities, and the cities required something no forager band ever needed: administration. To run a kingdom of strangers, fed by taxes of grain, rulers needed records that outlasted human memory. The next great invention of our species was therefore not a tool of wood or stone, but a tool of information: writing.

CHAPTER 7
Building Pyramids: Writing, Numbers and Bureaucracy

Farming created societies too large for anyone to hold in their head. A forager elder could remember every member of the band, every debt and every promise. A king ruling fifty thousand subjects, collecting taxes in grain from hundreds of villages, paying armies and work gangs, could not. The human brain is a wonderful organ, but it is a poor filing cabinet: memories fade, witnesses die, and disputes over who paid what become unresolvable. Kingdoms faced a problem of information before they faced any other, and the Sumerians of southern Mesopotamia solved it, sometime between 3500 and 3000 BC, by inventing writing.

It is worth pausing on what writing was invented for. Not poetry, not history, not prayer: accounting. The earliest written texts are tedious lists of rations, taxes and livestock, quantities of barley received and owed. The very first named individual in history, known from a clay tablet about 5,000 years old, is not a king or a prophet but a man called Kushim, apparently an accountant signing for deliveries of barley. Writing began as a partial script, a system of signs good for one kind of information, numbers and commodities, but unable to record a love poem. Only gradually did scripts become complete, able to represent spoken language in full. Egypt's hieroglyphs and Mesopotamia's cuneiform both travelled this road, pushed by the needs of administrators.

One quiet innovation mattered enormously: separating numbers from words. Oral language entangles them; you say "three sheep" as one phrase. Bureaucratic script treated quantities as a system of their own, with its own symbols and rules, and in doing so opened the door to mathematics as an independent discipline. The Mesopotamians developed a base-60 arithmetic whose fingerprints are still on our lives: sixty minutes in an hour, sixty seconds in a minute, 360 degrees in a circle. Every time you check the time, you are reading the legacy of Sumerian tax clerks.

Writing changed religion and myth as profoundly as it changed taxation. Oral stories are fluid; each storyteller adjusts the tale, and the myth evolves with the society. A written scripture freezes. Once the words of a god or a lawgiver were fixed on clay or parchment, they acquired an authority independent of any living voice, and a new kind of specialist arose to interpret the frozen text: the priest-scholar, the rabbi, the jurist. Debates that foragers settled by consensus now became arguments over what a document meant. Much of the intellectual history of the last three thousand years, theology, law, philosophy, commentary upon commentary, is the history of minds wrestling with inherited texts. Writing gave human memory a body outside the skull, and in doing so it changed what a mind is for: less a storehouse, more an index. It also made possible the long-distance state: an emperor in Babylon could issue the same order, in the same words, to officials a month's journey apart, and check their accounts years later.

The consequences of writing reached far beyond record-keeping. Archives made the state impersonal and durable: a tax demand no longer depended on a chief's memory but on a tablet in a storehouse, and it could be enforced years later by officials who had never met the taxpayer. Law codes could be fixed, published and applied uniformly across a kingdom. Bureaucracy, rule by the office and the file, was born, and with it a new way of thinking. Where foragers classified the world through rich narrative knowledge, bureaucrats classified it through tables, categories and forms, and began forcing messy reality to fit the boxes. A field that straddled two districts, a person who fit no census category: the form, not the world, usually won. We still live inside that mental habit every time we fill in a form or organise a spreadsheet.

Writing also created a new priesthood: scribes. Because cuneiform had hundreds of signs, mastering it took years of schooling, and the literate few became indispensable to every institution. For most of history, literacy remained the guarded skill of a tiny elite, which is one reason the myths of the powerful went so long unchallenged. Only with cheap printing, five thousand years after Kushim, would reading escape the scribal class, and by then writing had long since reshaped human thought itself. People who keep records think differently: more abstractly, more categorically, more in terms of lists and ledgers. Our modern minds, trained from childhood to store knowledge outside our heads, in books, files and now phones, are the heirs of the accountants of Sumer.

Administration on this scale required one more fiction, perhaps the most successful ever told. Bureaucracies could count grain, but trade between strangers across a growing world needed something that everyone, everywhere, would accept in exchange for real goods. That something was money, and its story shows the power of shared belief at its purest, because money works even between enemies who agree on nothing else.

CHAPTER 8
The Scent of Money

Imagine a pre-money economy. You grow apples and want shoes. You must find a shoemaker who happens to want apples, in the right quantity, today, before they rot. Economists call this the problem of the double coincidence of wants, and it strangles trade. Barter works within a village where everyone knows everyone's needs; it cannot support a complex economy. Money solved the problem, and money is best understood not as a material but as a system of trust, the most universal and efficient system of mutual trust ever devised. Anything can serve as money if people believe others will accept it: cowrie shells in Africa and Asia, salt, cattle, barley, cigarettes in prisoner-of-war camps, and today, electronic data.

The first money we know of was Sumerian barley money, around 3000 BC: a fixed measure of barley functioned as a unit of account, so wages and prices could be stated in it. Barley was bulky and perishable, though, and the breakthrough came around 640 BC in the kingdom of Lydia, in modern Turkey, with the first coins: pieces of gold and silver alloy stamped with a mark. The stamp was the point. It testified that an authority guaranteed the metal's weight and purity, so a stranger could accept the coin without scales or suspicion. Coins spread through the Mediterranean world, and the Roman denarius became so trusted that it circulated far beyond Rome's borders, among peoples who despised Rome itself.

Why does money work so well? Because it performs three services at once. It is a universal medium of conversion: apples into shoes, shoes into rent, rent into loyalty, anything into anything. It stores wealth: grain rots and cattle die, but coins and bank balances wait patiently for years. And it transports wealth: a fortune in wheat needs a fleet of carts; the same fortune in gold, or today in a password, fits in a pocket. Crucially, money's magic survives the collapse of every other shared belief. Christians and Muslims who agreed on nothing theological trusted the same gold dinars and ducats. Prisoners who shared no language traded in cigarettes. Money is the one story that enemies can tell together, because each person accepts it for a simple reason: everyone else does.

Trust in money can also collapse, and the collapses are instructive. Rulers chronically short of funds discovered they could debase coinage, mixing in copper while stamping the same face, and pocket the difference. The result was inflation and, when discovered, a refusal of the bad coins: bad money may drive out good in daily use, but it destroys the story that money depends on. China, which invented paper money under the Song dynasty, learned the same lesson when over-issued notes lost their value. Modern central banking, for all its complexity, is essentially a machine for protecting this one fiction: that the tokens everyone accepts today will still be accepted tomorrow. When that belief failed in Germany in 1923 or Zimbabwe in 2008, societies did not merely become poorer; they discovered, terrifyingly, that the most solid-seeming part of their world had been a story all along, and the story had stopped being believed. Money's triumph is that it usually survives even this: new currencies are issued, the story is retold with fresh symbols, and within a few years strangers are trusting paper again, because the alternative, a world of barter and suspicion, is worse.

This should not be mistaken for mere convention, like driving on the left. Money is a psychological creation of immense power, and it has repeatedly proven stronger than political authority. When empires fell, their coins often kept circulating. When governments debased currencies, people fled to foreign money or gold, whatever the law said. Kings could command armies, but they could not command trust. In our own time, the overwhelming majority of money is not even metal or paper; it is data in bank computers, figures that appear and disappear at the speed of light. The total value of this electronic money vastly exceeds all physical cash in existence. We have completed money's journey from barley to pure information, and it still works, because the story still holds.

Money also has a darker property worth noting before we move on. Because it converts everything into everything, it tends to convert things that perhaps should not be for sale: land, labour, loyalty, and eventually human beings. And because trust in money requires no shared values, money corrodes other values: a community bound by honour or religion may refuse certain trades, but the logic of money recognises no such refusals. Two forces in history have pushed against money's corrosion and simultaneously ridden its power: the empire, which unified peoples politically, and the universal religions, which claimed to unify them spiritually. Together with money, these three, economic, political and religious orders, gradually bound humankind into a single world.

CHAPTER 9
Imperial Visions

An empire is a political order with two defining features: it rules a large number of distinct peoples, each with its own identity and territory, and it has flexible borders and a potentially unlimited appetite for more. Empires are not a rare aberration in history; they are its default large-scale condition. For most of the past 2,500 years, most humans have lived in empires. The first known empire, the Akkadian Empire of Sargon the Great, was founded around 2250 BC. Sargon began as a minor official, seized a city, and then conquered all of Mesopotamia, boasting that his armies had won thirty-four battles. He set the template that Cyrus of Persia, Alexander, Rome, the caliphates, the Mongols, Spain, Britain and many others would follow.

Conquest is brutal, and empires were born in blood. Yet empires also did something their founders rarely intended: they dissolved the cultures they conquered into larger hybrids. An empire spreads a common language for administration, common laws, common money, roads and calendars, and over generations the conquered adopt these tools and make them their own. When the British left India, they left behind English, parliamentary ideas, cricket and railways, which independent India kept. The Arabic language and Islam spread from Spain to Iraq not by Arab numbers but by imperial institutions. Spanish America speaks Spanish. The cultural map of the modern world was drawn less by ancient nations than by ancient empires, and almost everyone alive today speaks an imperial language, uses imperial technologies and lives within borders some empire drew.

Empires also changed the moral geometry of "us" and "them". A tribe divides humanity into us and the barbarians, permanently. An empire, at least in its mature ideology, claims to rule for the benefit of all its subjects and treats its order as potentially universal. Cyrus the Great presented himself not as the enslaver of nations but as their liberator and guarantor of their customs. Rome eventually extended citizenship to every free man in the empire. The conquered were still exploited, often savagely, but the imperial story said the boundary between ruler and ruled was temporary and administrative, not eternal and natural. That story, repeated over centuries, slowly taught humanity to imagine itself as one family under one order, an idea that outlived every particular empire.

Empires also fell, regularly, and their falls are as instructive as their rises. Overextension, succession crises, plague, fiscal exhaustion and the slow decay of the founding myth all played their parts. Yet notice what usually replaced a fallen empire: not a return to tiny independent tribes, but successor states that claimed the empire's mantle and imitated its institutions. After Rome fell in the west, Charlemagne, the Byzantines, the caliphs and eventually the tsars, whose very title derives from Caesar, all presented themselves as Rome's heirs. The imperial idea proved harder to kill than any empire. Even today's republics govern through imperial machinery: centralised bureaucracies, standing armies, uniform law and currencies spanning many peoples.

It is fashionable, and partly right, to judge empires as engines of oppression. But the honest historian must add two complications. First, the cultural legacy of empires cannot be cleanly separated into "authentic" native culture and "imposed" foreign culture: after a few generations, the imperial culture is the native culture of real people, and attempts to purify it usually destroy living traditions in the name of dead ones. Second, the alternative to empire in most eras was not a garden of free nations but fragmentation into warring statelets. The unification of humankind proceeded largely through imperial violence, which is an uncomfortable truth, but a truth.

Look at the modern world with this lens and a surprise emerges: there are almost no nations that are not, in structure, miniature empires. The roughly two hundred states of today are nearly all multi-ethnic, multi-lingual and multi-religious, held together by a central government, a shared currency and a founding story that claims the arrangement is natural and eternal. France, India, Nigeria, Indonesia, Brazil: each rules many peoples under one law, which is precisely the definition of empire. The nation-state did not replace the empire. It is the empire, rebranded with a democratic myth and a flag behind which the old machinery continues to run. Recognising this is not cynicism; it is simply the long view, and it explains why dismantling an empire so rarely produces the free garden of independent nations that its opponents promised and its subjects imagined.

By the start of the modern era, three great universal orders were knitting the planet together. Money provided the economic order: a single global system of trust. Empires provided the political order: ever-larger states ruling ever-more-diverse peoples. And universal religions provided the third order, claiming to speak not for one tribe but for all humankind, under laws laid down by a superhuman authority. It is to religion, the most underestimated of the three, that we now turn.

CHAPTER 10
The Law of Religion

Religion can be defined as a human order founded on belief in a superhuman order: a set of laws and values that bind people together because they are believed to come from an authority above humanity. This definition matters because it explains religion's historical function. A social order is always fragile; people ask why they should obey its laws, respect its hierarchies, keep its promises. Religion answers: because a higher power commands it. The gods, or heaven, or the law of nature, stand guarantor for the human story, and that guarantee is what let large societies hold together without a policeman on every corner.

Ancient religions were mostly local and tolerant. A city's gods guarded that city; nobody imagined they demanded worship from foreigners, and polytheists could add a new god to the pantheon without crisis. The first universal and missionary religions changed the game. Christianity and Islam taught that there is one God for all humankind, that his truth is the only truth, and that believers have a duty to spread it. Such faiths were inherently expansionist, and their spread, by preaching, trade and the sword, unified vast regions under one story. Buddhism took a different path to universality: its founder sought liberation from suffering for all beings, and its core law, that suffering arises from craving, was offered as a fact about the universe rather than a god's command. It spread across Asia largely through monks and merchants.

Something remarkable happened to religion across Eurasia between roughly 800 and 200 BC, the period the philosopher Karl Jaspers called the Axial Age. In China, Confucius and Laozi taught ethics and harmony; in India, the Buddha and the Jain and Upanishadic thinkers questioned ritual and sought universal truths; in Persia, Zoroaster preached a cosmic struggle of good and evil; in the Levant, the Hebrew prophets demanded justice for the poor; in Greece, philosophers began to reason about nature and the good life. These movements arose independently, yet they share a direction: away from local cults concerned with rain and harvest, toward universal moral orders concerned with how all humans should live. Whether the driver was urbanisation, coinage, empire or alphabetic writing, the Axial Age laid the spiritual foundations on which the universal religions, and later the universal ideologies, were built.

Religion's unifying work was not only spiritual. Universal faiths carried law codes, calendars, charitable institutions, schools and art across continents, and gave strangers a shared moral vocabulary. A Muslim merchant from Baghdad and one from Cordoba could trust each other partly because both lived under the same sacred law. Monasteries preserved literacy; pilgrimages stitched regions together; religious festivals synchronised the year for millions. When we list the forces that made humankind one, the missionary religions stand beside money and empire, and in the lives of ordinary people they were usually the most intimate of the three.

The coin has a dark side, and honesty demands a look at it. Precisely because universal religions claim to possess the truth for everyone, they can license an intolerance that local cults rarely bothered with. Nobody was burned for refusing to worship a village river god; millions have been persecuted, converted by force or killed for heresy against a universal faith. The same story that taught strangers to trust one another also taught them to divide humanity into believers and infidels. Unification and persecution grew from the same root: a truth too important to leave unspread.

The definition of religion offered here has a provocative consequence: many modern ideologies qualify as religions in functional terms. Liberalism, communism and nationalism all rest on belief in a superhuman order, whether the laws of nature, the laws of history or the sacred destiny of the nation, and all demand faith in claims that cannot be proven in a laboratory, such as the equal worth of all humans. This is not a criticism. It is a recognition that human societies cannot run on facts alone; they need stories that tell people what is valuable and why they should cooperate. The modern world did not abolish religion's role. It filled that role with new creeds, some of which, like the humanist creeds celebrating human life, liberty and happiness, became the water in which we all swim, invisible precisely because they are everywhere.

Yet for all their unifying power, the old orders of money, empire and religion shared a limitation: they were built on knowledge their founders believed was complete. Priests and emperors claimed to possess the essential truths already. Around five hundred years ago, a different idea appeared in Europe, an idea that began by confessing ignorance, and it would prove more powerful than any empire or faith before it. It would join forces with empire and capital, redraw the map, fill the oceans with ships, and eventually put weapons of species-ending power into human hands. That idea was modern science.

CHAPTER 11
The Admission of Ignorance

Every culture before the modern age assumed that the important knowledge about the world was already known. If a vital question arose, the answer lay in scripture, tradition or the wisdom of the elders. A medieval Christian who wanted to know why spiders have eight legs would not count spider legs; he would consult an authority, confident that if the matter were important, the Bible or Aristotle would have addressed it, and if they had not, it could not matter much. The Scientific Revolution, beginning in Europe around 1500, was built on the opposite confession, one that sounds simple and was in fact revolutionary: we do not know. Not I personally, but we, collectively, humanity, do not know the most important things, and we are willing to admit it and go and look.

Modern science differs from all previous knowledge traditions in three ways. First, the willingness to admit ignorance, not as a failure but as a starting point. Second, the centrality of observation and mathematics: knowledge is built by gathering data and expressing patterns in mathematical models, rather than by contemplating ancient texts. Third, the purpose of knowledge: power. Science does not merely describe the world; it seeks new capacities, to heal diseases, build weapons, fly. The Latin motto of the revolution could be knowledge is power, and its test was brutally practical: a theory mattered if it let you do something you could not do before.

The confession of ignorance had an explosive cultural consequence. Premodern maps left no blank spaces; mapmakers filled unknown regions with monsters and imaginary kingdoms rather than admit a gap. In the decades after Columbus, European maps began to show honest emptiness: coastlines that stopped mid-ocean, interiors left white. Those blank spaces were an invitation. Between 1492 and the early twentieth century, European explorers, soldiers and merchants filled them in, conquering most of the planet in the process. The alliance between science and empire was intimate: empires funded expeditions expecting useful knowledge, and scientists hitched rides on warships, producing maps, botanical collections and intelligence that made further conquest possible. Captain Cook's voyages were scientific expeditions that also, not coincidentally, claimed Australia and New Zealand for Britain.

Why did this happen in Europe rather than in China or the Islamic world, which had led in science for centuries? The honest answer involves institutions and mindset more than genius. Europe by 1500 was a patchwork of competing states; a scientist or explorer refused in one capital could try the next, so no single authority could suppress innovation. European rulers, locked in permanent military competition, were hungry for any knowledge that promised advantage, and they built universities, academies and observatories to get it. Just as importantly, Europeans developed the peculiar belief that the future could be different from and better than the past, that unknown lands and unknown truths were worth fortunes to find. Empires elsewhere certainly conquered, but the systematic fusion of conquest with research was a European speciality of the era, and it paid off beyond all expectation.

It is equally important to see how science actually works day to day, because the method, not any single discovery, is the revolution. A scientist proposes a model of some corner of reality, derives predictions from it, and checks those predictions against observation and experiment. Other scientists, rivals with every incentive to find mistakes, repeat the work and attack the conclusions in journals and conferences. Theories that survive are kept provisionally; theories that fail are revised or discarded, however beloved. No earlier knowledge system built error-correction into its core. Scripture-based scholarship could refine interpretation but could not declare the founding text wrong. Science can, and regularly does, which is why its knowledge compounds while traditional wisdom mostly circulates. The admission of ignorance is therefore not humility theatre. It is the engine: because the map is known to be incomplete, expeditions are funded, instruments are built, and anomalies, the facts that do not fit, are treasured rather than explained away. The telescope and the microscope, the first great instruments, made the point viscerally: they revealed moons around Jupiter and worlds of creatures in a drop of water that no scripture had ever mentioned, proving by demonstration that reality contained multitudes the ancients had never dreamed of.

Science, however, has a weakness: it is expensive. Telescopes, laboratories, expeditions and universities require funding on a scale only states and wealthy institutions can supply, which means scientists must persuade politicians and bankers that their research serves the funder's story: national glory, military strength, economic growth. Pure science, pursued for curiosity alone, has always ridden on the back of these alliances. In the modern world the most consequential alliance was with a new economic creed that promised endless growth, a creed built on a radical new idea about the future: credit. Science taught Europe that tomorrow could surpass today. Capitalism turned that idea into a financial system.

CHAPTER 12
The Capitalist Creed

In premodern economies, credit was small, expensive and morally suspect. The reason was a deep assumption: the future would be much like the past, and the total wealth of the world was essentially fixed. If the pie never grows, then lending is a gamble on someone else's slice, interest is exploitation, and the prudent hoard rather than invest. Most religions agreed, condemning usury. Then, in the centuries after 1500, a new faith took hold in Europe: the future will be better than the present. Output will grow. And if the pie grows, credit becomes something entirely different, the mechanism by which today's savings build tomorrow's larger pie. Modern economics runs on this act of faith. Credit is trust in the future, made transferable.

The mechanics are elegant. Suppose a baker believes she could double her sales with a second oven, but has no money. A bank lends her the price of the oven, money it creates, in effect, against her future earnings, since banks lend far more than the cash deposited with them. She pays a builder, who deposits his fee in his own account, which the bank can lend again. The oven gets built, sales grow, the loan is repaid with interest, and everyone profits from wealth that did not exist when the loan was signed. Multiply this by millions and you have the modern world: an economy in which the total money supply is mostly promises, and the promises hold because growth keeps arriving to redeem them. When trust in the future collapses, as in 2008, the system seizes within days, which shows how much of capitalism is a shared story about tomorrow.

The story's fragility showed early. In the 1630s the Dutch, pioneers of finance, drove the price of single tulip bulbs to the level of Amsterdam townhouses before the market collapsed in weeks. In 1720 the Mississippi Bubble in France and the South Sea Bubble in Britain ruined thousands who had believed that paper claims could multiply forever. Each crash taught the same lesson: credit works only while trust holds, and trust is a mood as much as a calculation. Yet remarkably, the system did not die of its crises. Instead it built shock absorbers: central banks, deposit insurance, bankruptcy law, limited liability. Capitalism's genius was not avoiding failure but institutionalising recovery, so that the collapse of one story about the future no longer destroyed the capacity to tell new ones.

Adam Smith gave the creed its scripture in 1776. In The Wealth of Nations he argued that when a landowner or mill owner reinvests profits to expand production and hire more workers, the resulting growth benefits everyone, so private greed can be a public good. This was a startling moral inversion: for millennia, greed had been a sin and the rich were suspected of taking from the poor. Smith taught that the rich man who grows his capital is a benefactor, and that the economy is not a zero-sum struggle over a fixed pie but a rising tide. Capitalism added one commandment that distinguished it from mere commerce: profits must be reinvested in production, not squandered on palaces and pageantry. Smith's famous example makes the mechanism vivid: it is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest, and a well-ordered market is precisely a machine for harnessing that self-love to public benefit rather than merely condemning it. The capitalist is the person who ploughs money back into the business, and the system's heroes became not kings but entrepreneurs.

Honesty requires recording the creed's crimes alongside its miracles. Capitalism fused with empire produced the Atlantic slave trade, in which millions of Africans were transported in chains because investors in Amsterdam and London found sugar plantations profitable and never had to see them. It produced the rubber terror of the Congo and the opium forced on China. The free market, left entirely to itself, does not protect the weak; it prices them. The system worked, in the narrow sense of generating unprecedented growth, but its growth was watered with blood as well as credit. Modern societies still wrestle with the resulting tension: we depend on the capitalist engine for prosperity, and we build laws, unions and welfare states to fence in its cruelties.

Notice what capitalism and science share: both are bets on growth. Science promises that knowledge will grow; capitalism, that wealth will grow; and each funds the other in a feedback loop that has run for five hundred years. In 1500, global production was worth roughly 250 billion dollars in today's terms; today it is tens of trillions annually, and per person it has multiplied more than tenfold despite an eightfold increase in population. Something had to supply the energy for this explosion, because no amount of credit can bake bread without heat. In the eighteenth century, humanity learned to tap a new source of power, and the resulting industrial revolution transformed not just economies but the most intimate structures of human life: the family and the community.

CHAPTER 13
The Industrial Revolution and the Vanishing Family

Before industry, almost all energy was muscle energy, and muscle energy runs on photosynthesis: plants capture sunlight, animals and humans eat plants or plant-eaters, and the whole economy is capped by the harvest. The Industrial Revolution was, at its core, an energy revolution. Humans learned to convert heat into motion with the steam engine, and then to unlock the stored sunlight of coal, oil and gas, fossil fuels laid down over hundreds of millions of years. Suddenly production was limited not by land and weather but by machines and fuel. Factories replaced workshops, cities swelled, railways and steamships shrank the planet, and within two centuries the material basis of life, food, clothing, light, transport, had been utterly transformed.

The second act of the revolution compounded the first. Electricity lit factories that no longer depended on a single steam engine's belts and shafts, and let work continue through the night. Chemistry delivered fertilisers that multiplied harvests, and with them dyes, medicines and explosives. The internal combustion engine put motorised transport into ordinary hands, and the assembly line, perfected by Henry Ford, inverted the logic of craft: instead of skilled workers building whole products, unskilled workers repeated single motions as products flowed past. Mass production demanded mass consumption, and advertising arose to manufacture desire at the same scale as the factories manufactured goods. By the mid-twentieth century, the citizens of industrial societies lived amid a plenty, of food, clothing, furniture, entertainment, that their great-grandparents would have classed as fairy tale, and they had organised their entire lives around earning the money to buy it.

The revolution industrialised animals as ruthlessly as it industrialised cloth. For thousands of years, domestic animals lived within a web of relationships on farms, valued for milk, wool, labour and eggs across a lifetime. Industrial farming reduced them to production units in a system designed by engineers: calves separated from mothers at birth, chickens bred to grow so fast their legs break, sows confined in crates too small to turn around. Measured by evolutionary success, these species triumph; measured by the suffering of individuals, the industrial farm is among the darkest products of our ingenuity, the Agricultural Revolution's bargain brought to its logical extreme.

The deepest social casualty of the industrial age was quieter: the collapse of the family and the local community as the basic units of welfare. For millennia, family and community were a person's insurance company, hospital, pension fund, bank, police force and employment agency combined. If you fell sick, your family nursed you. If you fell poor, your community fed you, expecting help in return. If you were robbed, your kin pursued justice. These networks were often oppressive, especially to women and the young, but they caught people when they fell. Industrial society transferred their functions, one by one, to the state and the market. Wage labour let individuals survive without kin. Insurance replaced the helpful cousin; banks replaced the family loan; police and courts replaced the avenging uncle; state pensions replaced dutiful children; hospitals replaced the nursing mother.

The liberation was real: a person could now escape a tyrannical family, choose a spouse, a job, a city, a life. But the safety net woven from obligation and affection was replaced by services bought with money or claimed from bureaucracies, and something human went missing. The state can pay a pension, but it cannot sit by your bedside. Modern societies are filled with individuals who are legally free, materially richer than any farmer of 1700, and profoundly lonely. We filled the gap partly with imagined communities: the nation, the political movement, the fan base, the online network. These can inspire and entertain, but they rarely nurse you through a fever. The atomised individual, dependent on employers and officials, free and fragile at once, is industrial modernity's characteristic human product.

Industry also rewired time itself. Agricultural life followed seasons and daylight; industrial life follows the clock. Factories demanded synchronised shifts, railways demanded standard time across whole countries, and schools trained children in punctuality for the world of the timetable. We now plan life in units, hours, semesters, quarters, careers, that would bewilder a medieval peasant, and we experience a scarcity of time amid an abundance of goods that no previous generation would have believed. The machine age gave us more of almost everything except hours in the day and people at the table.

With production exploding and old bonds dissolving, a question that earlier ages rarely had the luxury to ask moved to the centre: are people actually happier? History had measured conquest, wealth and faith for thousands of years, but it had never systematically asked whether progress made its beneficiaries content. The answer, when psychologists and historians finally examined it, turned out to be far stranger than either optimists or pessimists expected.

CHAPTER 14
Happiness, and the Gods We Are Becoming

Consider two facts. A medieval peasant lived in a mud hut, drank unsafe water, and could expect several of his children to die. A modern office worker has antibiotics, heating, entertainment on demand and a supermarket. By every objective measure, the modern life is better. Yet it does not follow that the modern worker is happier, because happiness turns out to depend less on objective conditions than on the gap between conditions and expectations, and expectations adapt relentlessly. When conditions improve, expectations rise to meet them, and satisfaction stays roughly where it was. The peasant who expected little was not necessarily less content than the executive who expects much and is furious when the wifi fails.

Biology deepens the puzzle. Subjective wellbeing is governed substantially by our biochemical system, which evolution tuned not for contentment but for survival and reproduction. Pleasant sensations fade quickly, because an animal permanently satisfied would stop striving; unpleasant ones linger, because they signal danger. Studies of lottery winners and of people paralysed in accidents famously found that after an initial swing, both groups drift back toward their previous baseline of happiness. Our internal thermostat has a set point, differing by person and largely inherited, and life events move the needle less, and for less long, than we imagine. If this is right, then all the revolutions in this book, agricultural, industrial, scientific, may have changed our happiness far less than they changed our power, because they could not change the chemistry that judges them.

History adds a third factor: meaning. Humans appear to need a story that makes their lives significant, and they will endure astonishing hardship inside a story they believe, while crumbling in comfort inside one they do not. A medieval Christian who believed suffering purchased heaven experienced his hardships differently from a modern person who suspects life is pointless. This is why the question of happiness cannot be separated from the myths of earlier chapters. When the old stories weaken, prosperity alone does not fill the space where they stood.

And now the story turns toward the future, and toward the most consequential project our species has ever undertaken. Having conquered famine, plague and war to a degree our ancestors would have called godlike, humanity is setting itself the gods' own agenda: bliss, immortality and divinity. Longevity research seeks to treat ageing itself as a curable condition. Genetic engineering and cyborg engineering blur the line between organism and machine. Artificial intelligence raises the possibility of minds that are not organic at all, and of algorithms that know us better than we know ourselves. The likely heirs of Homo sapiens may be beings as different from us as we are from the Neanderthals, designed rather than evolved, and the decisions that create them will be taken in our lifetimes, by our laboratories, markets and governments.

The quest for immortality is as old as civilisation. The first hero of literature, Gilgamesh of Uruk, set out nearly 4,000 years ago to find the secret of eternal life after watching his friend die, and failed. Emperors of China drank mercury elixirs prepared by alchemists seeking the same prize, and died of the cure. What is new today is not the desire but the framing: death is increasingly described in laboratories as a technical problem, a set of malfunctions, cellular damage, genetic errors, protein misfolding, that might be repaired like faults in a machine. Serious money now follows the framing: technology billionaires fund longevity companies, and mainstream medicine has begun to classify ageing itself as a condition rather than a fate. Whether these projects succeed in decades or in centuries matters less than the shift they represent. For the first time, the species that invented gods is spending its surplus trying to become one, armed not with prayer but with venture capital and gene sequencing. And the same tools promise the third item on the divine agenda, creation itself: organisms designed from code, ecosystems engineered, perhaps minds built to order.

This is where the whole history converges. The animal of no significance learned to tell stories, and the stories built farms, cities, money, empires and sciences that made it mighty. But the same history warns us: again and again, from the wheat field to the factory farm, we gained power without gaining wisdom, and the beings inside our systems paid for our success. We are now acquiring the power to redesign life itself, including our own minds and desires, while still not knowing what we truly want. There is nothing more dangerous, the story of sapiens suggests, than gods who are dissatisfied and irresponsible, who wield the power of creation without a clear vision of what to create. The most important question in the world today is therefore not what we can do. It is what we should want to become.
